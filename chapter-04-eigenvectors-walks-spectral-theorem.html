4. Eigenvectors, walks and the spectral theorem

A walk through the five-device network can revisit a device or retrace a link. After eight steps from vertex $0$, there are 748 ways to return to $0$ and 304 ways to end at the pendant vertex $4$. Matrix powers count them. Eigenvectors explain how those powers separate into independent scalar growth factors.

Four recordings develop eigenpairs, adjacency powers, orthogonal diagonalization and the Rayleigh quotient. Their complete machine transcripts were read sequentially. We retain that spine, supply the real-eigenpair existence step in the spectral proof, and solve the triangle, four-cycle and principal-minor exercises. Explicit graph matrices, calculations and error checks are original constructions. No complete visual board transcription is claimed.

Unless stated otherwise, graphs remain finite, simple and undirected. Their adjacency matrices are real and symmetric. We first allow a general square matrix $M$ to explain which conclusions require symmetry. Its size is $n\times n$ with $n\ge1$; vectors are columns. The main spectral theorem concerns real vectors and real symmetric matrices.

4.1 An eigenvector keeps its line under a transformation 02:02

An eigenpair is a scalar $\lambda$ and a nonzero vector $x$ satisfying $Mx=\lambda x$. The vector is an eigenvector and the scalar its eigenvalue. A positive real eigenvalue preserves direction, a negative one reverses it, and zero sends the nonzero vector to zero. All three possibilities keep the output on the input's line. The zero vector is excluded because $M0=\lambda0$ would otherwise hold for every scalar.

If $x$ is an eigenvector, then $cx$ is one for every nonzero scalar $c$, with the same eigenvalue. The full eigenspace $E_\lambda=\ker(M-\lambda I)$ includes the zero vector so that it is a vector space; its nonzero members are the eigenvectors. A combination of eigenvectors for one eigenvalue is again an eigenvector only if the combination is nonzero. Cancellation can produce zero.

The exchange matrix $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ swaps two coordinates. It sends $(1,1)^T$ to itself and $(1,-1)^T$ to its negative, giving eigenvalues $1,-1$. It sends $(1,2)^T$ to $(2,1)^T$, which is not a scalar multiple: the first coordinate would require multiplier $2$ and the second $1/2$. A vector need not be an eigenvector merely because its image can be calculated.

The identity $I_n$ has only one distinct eigenvalue, $1$, and every nonzero vector is an eigenvector. By contrast, the real quarter-turn $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has no real eigenvector. Its equations $-x_2=\lambda x_1$ and $x_1=\lambda x_2$ give $\lambda^2=-1$ for a nonzero solution. Over $\mathbb C$ the eigenvalues are $i,-i$, with eigenvectors $(1,-i)^T$ and $(1,i)^T$. The scalar field is part of the statement, not an optional afterthought.

4.2 A determinant locates eigenvalues but not an eigenbasis 14:17

A scalar $t$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $(M-tI)x=0$ has a nonzero solution, equivalently when that matrix is singular. We use the monic characteristic polynomial $p_M(t)=\det(tI-M)$. The alternative convention $\det(M-tI)$ differs by the constant factor $(-1)^n$ and has the same roots.

This polynomial has degree $n$: its leading term chooses $t$ from each diagonal entry. Over $\mathbb C$, the fundamental theorem of algebra gives $n$ roots counted with repetition. The algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root; the geometric multiplicity is $\dim E_\lambda$. Repeated roots do not by themselves supply independent eigenvectors.

For $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $p_J(t)=(t-1)^2$ but $(J-I)x=0$ forces $x_2=0$. There is only a one-dimensional eigenspace. The matrix cannot have two independent eigenvectors. In fact, writing $J=I+N$ with $N^2=0$ gives $J^k=I+kN=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ for nonnegative integers $k$. Keeping only the repeated scalar power $1^k$ would miss the growing off-diagonal entry. Finding eigenvalues and finding a usable basis are different tasks.

If $n$ independent eigenvectors are available as the columns of an invertible matrix $V$, and their eigenvalues form the diagonal matrix $\Lambda$, then $MV=V\Lambda$. Multiplying by $V^{-1}$ gives $M=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ and $M^k=V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$. The inverse is justified by independence, not by merely assembling $n$ vectors with eigenvector labels. For $k=0$, $M^0=I$ and every diagonal entry of $\Lambda^0$ is $1$, including a slot whose eigenvalue is zero. Negative powers require invertibility and are not used for walk counting.

4.3 Distinct eigenvalues separate directions 18:43

Independence theorem. Eigenvectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ for pairwise distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$ are independent, even without symmetry. Induct on $k$. A single nonzero vector is independent. Given $\sum_{i=1}^k c_iv_i=0$, apply $M-\lambda_kI$. The result is $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_k)v_i=0$. By induction every coefficient in this shorter combination is zero. Distinctness gives $c_i=0$ for $i<k$, and the original combination then forces $c_k=0$.

Orthogonality theorem. If $M$ is real symmetric and $Mv=\lambda v$, $Mw=\mu w$ for real distinct eigenvalues, then $v^Tw=0$. Indeed, $v^TMw=\mu v^Tw$ while symmetry gives $v^TMw=(Mv)^Tw=\lambda v^Tw$. Subtraction yields $(\mu-\lambda)v^Tw=0$. This proof does not say that two arbitrary eigenvectors in the same eigenspace are orthogonal. For example, any two nonorthogonal nonzero vectors are eigenvectors of the identity. Within a repeated eigenspace, one may choose an orthonormal basis instead.

A real square matrix $Q$ is orthogonal if $Q^TQ=I$. Its columns are an orthonormal basis. The equation implies its kernel is zero, since $\|Qx\|^2=x^TQ^TQx=\|x\|^2$. Because it is square, it is invertible; hence $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ and also $QQ^T=I$. Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and inner products. The word does not mean their columns are merely perpendicular with arbitrary lengths.

4.4 Adjacency powers count walks, including repetitions 05:24

Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a graph: $A_{ij}=1$ if $i,j$ are joined and zero otherwise. For every integer $k\ge0$, $(A^k)_{ij}$ equals the number of length-$k$ walks from $i$ to $j$. Connectedness is unnecessary. If the vertices lie in different components, the number and the corresponding entry are both zero.

Proof. At $k=0$, there is one empty walk from a vertex to itself and none to another vertex, giving $I$. Suppose the assertion holds at $k$. A length-$k+1$ walk ending at $j$ has a unique penultimate vertex $u$. Remove its last edge to get a length-$k$ walk from $i$ to $u$; conversely any such walk extends to $j$ exactly when $A_{uj}=1$. These classes are disjoint because the penultimate vertex is specified. Therefore the count is $\sum_u(A^k)_{iu}A_{uj}=(A^{k+1})_{ij}$. This proves the induction.

Repeated vertices and repeated edges are allowed. The two-step walk $i\to u\to i$ is counted even though it is not a simple path or cycle. In particular $(A^2)_{ii}=d_i$, and $\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=\sum_id_i=2m$. Here $\operatorname{tr}$ means the sum of diagonal entries. For $i\ne j$, $(A^2)_{ij}$ counts their common neighbours, not the number of all possible paths between them.

In our established edge order, the graph has edges $01,12,23,03,02,24$. Vertices $1$ and $3$ have common neighbours $0,2$, so there are two two-step walks between them. Vertex $4$ can return to itself in two steps only as $4\to2\to4$, giving diagonal entry one. Eight-step counts will be much larger; the code below checks their exact values independently using endpoint recursion.

4.5 Why every real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis 12:23

Spectral theorem. A real matrix $M$ is symmetric if and only if $M=Q\Lambda Q^T$ for some real orthogonal $Q$ and real diagonal $\Lambda$. The columns of $Q$ are an orthonormal eigenbasis, and its diagonal entries are the corresponding eigenvalues. Repeated eigenvalues are allowed.

The central proof needs a real eigenvector to begin its induction. We establish that existence instead of assuming a characteristic-polynomial root is real. On the real unit sphere, the continuous function $f(x)=x^TMx$ attains a maximum because the sphere is compact. Let $v$ be a maximizing unit vector and set $\lambda=v^TMv$. For any $w\perp v$, consider

$$g(t)=\frac{(v+tw)^TM(v+tw)}{1+t^2\|w\|^2}.$$

The denominator normalizes $v+tw$ to unit length. Thus $g$ has a maximum at zero, and differentiation gives $g'(0)=2w^TMv=0$, using symmetry. Decompose $Mv=\lambda v+r$, where $r\perp v$. Taking $w=r$ yields $0=r^TMv=\|r\|^2$, so $r=0$. Therefore $Mv=\lambda v$ with a real eigenvalue and real nonzero eigenvector. No spectral theorem has been used in this existence step.

Now induct on dimension. The result is immediate for a real $1\times1$ matrix. Given the unit eigenvector $v$ above, its orthogonal complement $H=v^\perp$ is invariant under $M$: if $w\in H$, then $v^TMw=(Mv)^Tw=\lambda v^Tw=0$. Choose any orthonormal basis of $H$. In that basis the restricted linear map is represented by a real symmetric matrix of order $n-1$, because inner products $u^TMw$ and $(Mu)^Tw$ agree. The induction hypothesis supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis of this restriction. Its vectors remain eigenvectors of the full map since $H$ is invariant. Adjoining $v$ gives $n$ orthonormal eigenvectors of $M$.

Put those vectors into $Q$. The column equations give $MQ=Q\Lambda$, hence $M=Q\Lambda Q^T$. Conversely, transposing $Q\Lambda Q^T$ gives the same matrix because the real diagonal matrix is symmetric. This proves both directions. It also proves that all roots of the characteristic polynomial of a real symmetric matrix are real: $\det(tI-M)=\det(Q(tI-\Lambda)Q^T)=\prod_i(t-\lambda_i)$.

The proof handles repeated eigenvalues through orthogonal complements; it does not need every eigenvalue to be distinct. An eigenspace of dimension greater than one admits many orthonormal bases, so numerical eigenvectors are not unique. The theorem is a real-symmetric statement. A complex symmetric matrix is not automatically covered just by replacing real entries with complex ones; the analogous Hermitian theorem uses conjugate transpose and is outside this proof.

4.6 Spectral projectors turn powers into scalar powers 01:21

Write the unit eigenvectors as $q_1,\ldots,q_n$. Orthogonality gives $q_i^Tq_j=\delta_{ij}$, where the symbol is one for equal indices and zero otherwise. Consequently

$$M=\sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^T,\qquad I=\sum_iq_iq_i^T,\qquad M^k=\sum_i\lambda_i^kq_iq_i^T\quad(k\ge1).$$

The matrix $q_iq_i^T$ is the orthogonal projection onto that vector's line. Products of distinct such projections vanish, while each squares to itself. This proves the power formula directly. At $k=0$, use the displayed identity expansion; equivalently assign every scalar zeroth power the value one in this matrix-power convention. For a repeated eigenvalue, summing its line projectors gives the projection onto the entire eigenspace, independent of the selected orthonormal basis.

For adjacency, this yields the local walk formula $(A^k)_{uv}=\sum_i\lambda_i^kq_i(u)q_i(v)$. Eigenvalues alone determine the total trace, but local entries also need eigenvector coordinates. Since each unit vector gives $\operatorname{tr}(q_iq_i^T)=1$,

$$\operatorname{tr}(M^k)=\sum_i\lambda_i^k.$$

Consider the path $0-1-2$ as a small separate example. Its adjacency matrix has eigenvalues $\sqrt2,0,-\sqrt2$ and corresponding unit eigenvectors

$$q_+=\left(\frac12,\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac12\right)^T,\quad q_0=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},0,-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)^T,\quad q_-=\left(\frac12,-\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac12\right)^T.$$

Direct multiplication checks all three eigen-equations and their orthonormality. For $k\ge1$, the endpoint return count is $((\sqrt2)^k+(-\sqrt2)^k)/4$. It is zero for odd $k$ and $2^{r-1}$ for $k=2r$, $r\ge1$. The zero-length return count is separately one. Endpoint-to-middle walks have count $((\sqrt2)^k-(-\sqrt2)^k)/(2\sqrt2)$, which is $2^r$ when $k=2r+1$ and zero when $k$ is even. The zero eigenvalue's contribution at $k=0$ is necessary to recover the identity.

Diagonalization does not make scalar exponentiation or the initial eigensystem free. Repeated squaring also computes $A^k$ with only $O(\log k)$ matrix multiplications. Exact walk counts can have many bits; floating eigenpairs can lose accuracy when large powers amplify errors or terms nearly cancel. The spectral representation is an exact mathematical identity. Its numerical evaluation must be distinguished from exact integer counting.

4.7 Closed walks reveal triangles and four-cycles 21:29

A closed three-step walk in a simple loopless graph must use three distinct vertices before returning: any earlier repetition would require a loop on one of its steps. It therefore traverses a triangle. Each triangle gives six such walks, from three starting vertices and two traversal directions. If $N_3$ is the number of triangles,

$$N_3=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^3)}6=\frac16\sum_i\lambda_i^3.$$

A four-step closed walk can retrace an edge, so dividing its trace by eight without removing degeneracies is wrong. Classify it by its number of distinct vertices. With two vertices it alternates along one edge, giving two choices of start per edge, hence $2m$ walks. With three vertices the repeated pair of positions in the four-step sequence must be the starting and second vertex, or the first and third vertex: adjacent repetitions would require a loop. In either case its two used edges form a wedge with one central vertex and two distinct neighbours. For each unordered neighbour pair at a centre there are four walks: two starting at the centre with the neighbour order exchanged, and one starting at each endpoint. Thus this class contributes $4\sum_v\binom{d_v}{2}$. With four distinct vertices it traverses a four-cycle, giving eight walks per cycle: four starts and two directions.

These classes are disjoint and exhaustive in a simple graph. A chord between two cycle vertices does not invalidate the cycle; we count ordinary four-cycles, not necessarily induced ones. Therefore

$$N_4=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^4)-2m-4\sum_v\binom{d_v}{2}}8=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i^4-2\sum_vd_v^2+2m}{8}.$$

For the running graph, $m=6$, the degree list is $3,2,4,2,1$, and the wedge sum is $3+1+6+1+0=11$. Direct powers give traces $\operatorname{tr}(A^3)=12$ and $\operatorname{tr}(A^4)=64$. Hence there are two triangles and $(64-12-44)/8=1$ four-cycle. The pendant vertex contributes a backtracking walk but cannot belong to a simple cycle. The two triangles do not make an extra four-cycle beyond the square.

4.8 Principal minors are symmetric sums of eigenvalues 31:04

For an index set $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$, a principal submatrix $M[S,S]$ keeps the same row and column indices, in increasing order. Define $e_k(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ as the sum of all products of $k$ eigenvalues taken at distinct index positions; repeated numerical eigenvalues are still separate positions. Set $e_0=1$. Then

$$e_k(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)=\sum_{|S|=k}\det M[S,S].$$

This identity applies to any square matrix over $\mathbb C$, with algebraic multiplicities, even if it has no eigenbasis. Here is a determinant proof. Expand $\det(tI-M)$ by permutations, and within each factor choose either the $t\delta_{ij}$ term or the $-M_{ij}$ term. If exactly the index positions in $S$ choose entries from $-M$, every index outside $S$ must be fixed by the permutation to contribute a nonzero $t$ term. The remaining permutation acts on $S$, and summing its signed products gives $\det M[S,S]$. Thus

$$p_M(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^kt^{n-k}\sum_{|S|=k}\det M[S,S].$$

Factoring the same monic polynomial as $\prod_i(t-\lambda_i)$ gives coefficient $(-1)^ke_k$ at $t^{n-k}$. Comparing coefficients proves the claim. There is one empty principal submatrix at $k=0$, with determinant one. At $k=1$ the identity says $\sum_i\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}M$; at $k=n$ it says $\prod_i\lambda_i=\det M$.

For our adjacency matrix the characteristic polynomial is $t^5-6t^3-4t^2+2t$. In particular $e_2=-6$: every selected adjacent vertex pair contributes determinant $-1$ and every nonadjacent pair zero. The coefficient $-4t^2$ gives $e_3=4$, the sum of the two triangle minors, each equal to $2$. These are exact structural checks; the decimal eigenvalues are unnecessary for them.

Apply $k=n-1$ to the Laplacian, whose eigenvalues we now label in increasing order $0=\nu_1\le\nu_2\le\cdots\le\nu_n$. There is at least one zero eigenvalue from the constant vector, and all are nonnegative by the energy form. Every principal cofactor equals $\tau(G)$, so the principal-minor sum is $n\tau(G)$. In the eigenvalue sum only the product omitting $\nu_1$ can be nonzero, giving

$$\tau(G)=\frac1n\prod_{i=2}^n\nu_i.$$

If the graph is disconnected and $n\ge2$, another zero eigenvalue makes the product zero, as it should. At $n=1$, the empty product is one, also correct. For the running graph, direct multiplication verifies eigenvectors proportional to $(1,1,1,1,1)$, $(-1,-1,0,-1,3)$, $(0,-1,0,1,0)$, $(-2,1,0,1,0)$ and $(1,1,-4,1,1)$, with eigenvalues $0,1,2,4,5$ respectively. They are pairwise orthogonal. Thus the count is $1\cdot2\cdot4\cdot5/5=8$. This spectral form is consistent with the cofactor theorem in Brouwer–Haemers, §1.3.5; the coefficient derivation above supplies the connection explicitly.

4.9 Rayleigh quotients are weighted averages of eigenvalues 01:03

For nonzero real $x$, define the Rayleigh quotient $R_M(x)=x^TMx/(x^Tx)$. It is unchanged by nonzero scaling, so optimizing over all nonzero vectors is equivalent to optimizing $x^TMx$ on the unit sphere. For a real symmetric $M$, order eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n$ and expand $x=\sum_i\alpha_iq_i$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis. Then

$$x^Tx=\sum_i\alpha_i^2,\quad x^TMx=\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2,\quad R_M(x)=\sum_iw_i\lambda_i,\qquad w_i=\frac{\alpha_i^2}{\sum_j\alpha_j^2}.$$

The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. Thus $\lambda_n\le R_M(x)\le\lambda_1$. Both endpoints are attained by their eigenvectors, proving $\min_{x\ne0}R_M(x)=\lambda_n$ and $\max_{x\ne0}R_M(x)=\lambda_1$. The variational existence argument in Section 4.5 established an initial maximizing eigenvector before diagonalization; the present basis expansion describes every value and all equality cases after diagonalization. The reasoning is not circular.

For our adjacency matrix, take $x=\mathbf1$. The numerator is $2m=12$ and the denominator $n=5$, so $R_A(\mathbf1)=12/5=2.4$. This is a lower bound on the largest eigenvalue, not automatically its value. The degree list is not constant, so $A\mathbf1=(3,2,4,2,1)^T$ is not proportional to $\mathbf1$. The equality criterion below proves the bound is strict. Numerical eigenvalues give a largest value about $2.685544$, consistent with it.

4.10 Equality identifies a whole extreme eigenspace 17:19

The exact deficit from the largest eigenvalue is

$$\lambda_1-R_M(x)=\frac{\sum_i(\lambda_1-\lambda_i)\alpha_i^2}{\|x\|^2}.$$

Every summand is nonnegative. Equality is possible precisely when every coefficient for $\lambda_i<\lambda_1$ vanishes. The nonzero vector $x$ then lies in $E_{\lambda_1}$ and satisfies $Mx=\lambda_1x$. If the largest eigenvalue is repeated, any nonzero combination within that eigenspace is an extremizer. There is no justification for choosing one privileged vector. The minimum has the analogous characterization.

An interior eigenvalue has no such equality test. For $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,1,0)$ and $x=(1,0,1)^T$, $R_M(x)=1$, equal to an eigenvalue, but $Mx=(2,0,0)^T\ne x$. The weighted average reaches an interior value by balancing other eigenvalues. The nonnegative-deficit argument depends on using an extreme.

An independently derived error check is also useful. For a unit approximate eigenvector $x$ and a real scalar $\mu$, let $r=Mx-\mu x$ be its residual. Expansion in the eigenbasis gives $\|r\|^2=\sum_i(\lambda_i-\mu)^2\alpha_i^2$, while $\sum_i\alpha_i^2=1$. Hence at least one eigenvalue obeys $|\lambda_i-\mu|\le\|r\|$. A small residual locates some eigenvalue; it does not by itself identify which repeated or clustered direction the vector represents. Numerical residuals computed in floating arithmetic are checks, not interval-certified error bounds unless rounding is also controlled.

4.11 Exact walk counts and approximate spectral reconstruction

The program builds the running graph, compares exact matrix powers with a separate endpoint recursion, checks the cycle formulas and every characteristic coefficient against principal minors, and verifies the exact Laplacian eigenbasis. NumPy's symmetric eigensolver then supplies an approximate adjacency decomposition; residuals and the reconstructed eighth power are checked against exact results. Both SymPy and NumPy are required. No files or network resources are used.

from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np
from sympy import Matrix, prod, Rational

edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (0, 3), (0, 2), (2, 4)]
n, m = 5, len(edges)
a, d = Matrix.zeros(n), Matrix.zeros(n)
neighbours = [[] for _ in range(n)]
for u, v in edges:
    a[u, v] = a[v, u] = 1
    d[u, u] += 1
    d[v, v] += 1
    neighbours[u].append(v)
    neighbours[v].append(u)
lap = d-a
counts = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]
for _ in range(8):
    counts = [sum(counts[u] for u in neighbours[v]) for v in range(n)]
assert counts == list((a**8)[0, :])
triangles = int((a**3).trace()/6)
wedges = sum(len(row)*(len(row)-1)//2 for row in neighbours)
four_cycles = int(((a**4).trace()-2*m-4*wedges)/8)
assert triangles == 2 and four_cycles == 1
coefficients = a.charpoly().all_coeffs()
for k in range(n+1):
    minor_sum = sum(a.extract(s, s).det()
                    for s in combinations(range(n), k))
    assert coefficients[k] == (-1)**k * minor_sum
vectors = [Matrix(v) for v in [(1, 1, 1, 1, 1), (-1, -1, 0, -1, 3),
           (0, -1, 0, 1, 0), (-2, 1, 0, 1, 0), (1, 1, -4, 1, 1)]]
spectrum = [0, 1, 2, 4, 5]
for i, vector in enumerate(vectors):
    assert lap*vector == spectrum[i]*vector
    assert all(vector.dot(vectors[j]) == 0 for j in range(i))
trees = Rational(prod(spectrum[1:]), n)
assert trees == lap.minor_submatrix(2, 2).det() == 8
real_a = np.array(a, dtype=float)
values, q = np.linalg.eigh(real_a)
residual = np.max(np.abs(real_a @ q - q @ np.diag(values)))
reconstruction = q @ np.diag(values**8) @ q.T
spectral_check = residual < 1e-12 and np.allclose(
    reconstruction, np.array(a**8, dtype=float), rtol=0, atol=1e-8)
assert spectral_check
print('eight-step endpoint counts from 0:', counts)
print('trace A2 / A3 / A4:', (a**2).trace(), (a**3).trace(), (a**4).trace())
print('triangles / four-cycles:', triangles, '/', four_cycles)
print('all principal-minor coefficient checks: True')
print('exact Laplacian spectrum:', spectrum)
print('spectral tree count:', trees)
print('approximate spectral reconstruction and residual checks:', bool(spectral_check))
eight-step endpoint counts from 0: [748, 576, 832, 576, 304]
trace A2 / A3 / A4: 12 12 64
triangles / four-cycles: 2 / 1
all principal-minor coefficient checks: True
exact Laplacian spectrum: [0, 1, 2, 4, 5]
spectral tree count: 8
approximate spectral reconstruction and residual checks: True

The independent endpoint recursion partitions walks by their last vertex at each step, using Python integer sums. Its equality with the exact matrix power checks the graph indexing. The characteristic-minor tests include the empty and full index sets. The approximate eigensolver is tested through reconstruction, not by demanding identical eigenvector signs. Those signs are arbitrary, and repeated eigenspaces admit rotations of their basis.

4.12 Problems with complete solutions

  1. Nonzero is essential. For the exchange matrix, list all real eigenvectors for each eigenvalue. Is the sum of a $+1$ eigenvector and a $-1$ eigenvector necessarily an eigenvector? What about the zero combination inside one eigenspace?

    Solution

    The $+1$ eigenvectors are $c(1,1)^T$ and the $-1$ eigenvectors $c(1,-1)^T$, with $c\ne0$. Taking their sum with $c=1$ gives $(2,0)^T$, whose image is $(0,2)^T$, not a multiple, so it is not an eigenvector. More generally, a nonzero combination of two independent distinct-eigenvalue vectors with both coefficients nonzero cannot be an eigenvector: comparing coefficients in its eigen-equation would require one scalar equal to both eigenvalues. Inside one eigenspace, a linear combination stays in that eigenspace, but a zero result is not an eigenvector.

  2. Repeated root, missing direction. Find the eigenspace of $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Verify the eighth power and explain why pretending $J=QIQ^T$ is impossible.

    Solution

    The equation $(J-I)x=0$ gives $x_2=0$, so $E_1=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)^T\}$. The characteristic root1 has algebraic multiplicity2 but geometric multiplicity1. Since $N=J-I$ squares to zero, binomial expansion gives $J^8=I+8N=\begin{pmatrix}1&8\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. If an orthogonal $Q$ satisfied $J=QIQ^T$, the right side would be $QQ^T=I$, contrary to the off-diagonal entry. The real spectral theorem requires symmetry, which $J$ lacks.

  3. Count using a spectral basis. In the three-vertex path, find the number of six-step walks from endpoint0 back to0 and the number of seven-step walks from0 to the middle. Explain the parity obstruction.

    Solution

    The return formula at $k=6=2\cdot3$ gives $2^{3-1}=4$. The endpoint-to-middle formula at $k=7=2\cdot3+1$ gives $2^3=8$. Every step switches between the middle and the endpoint set. Consequently endpoint return requires an even number of steps, while endpoint-to-middle requires odd length. At each middle departure one chooses either endpoint; those choices account for the powers of two. A length-zero return is one and must not be assigned the even positive-length formula with $r=0$.

  4. Remove false four-cycles. A star has one centre and $r\ge1$ leaves. Compute its closed-four-walk trace combinatorially and use the four-cycle formula to show $N_4=0$. Why would dividing the trace by eight fail?

    Solution

    At the centre, a four-step closed walk chooses a leaf for the first excursion and a leaf for the second, giving $r^2$ walks. At each particular leaf, the walk goes to the centre, chooses any of $r$ leaves, returns to the centre and then to the starting leaf, giving $r$ walks. Summing over all $r$ starting leaves adds $r^2$, so the trace is $2r^2$. Here $m=r$ and the only nonzero wedge term is $\binom r2$ at the centre. The numerator is $2r^2-2r-4\binom r2=0$. All closed walks retrace edges; none uses four distinct vertices. Even the case $r=1$ has trace2 despite having only two vertices, so trace/8 cannot count its cycles.

  5. Read graph data from coefficients. A simple graph on five vertices has adjacency characteristic polynomial $t^5-6t^3-4t^2+2t$. Recover its number of edges and triangles. Do these two recovered counts alone determine the graph up to relabeling?

    Solution

    The coefficient of $t^3$ is $e_2=-6$ because $(-1)^2=1$. Each edge contributes a two-vertex principal determinant-1, so there are6edges. The coefficient of $t^2$ is $-e_3=-4$, giving $e_3=4$. A three-vertex principal adjacency determinant is2 for a triangle and0 otherwise, so there are2triangles. The two counts alone do not determine the graph. A different graph consists of triangles $012$ and $034$ sharing only vertex $0$. It also has five vertices, six edges and two triangles, but its degree multiset is $\{4,2,2,2,2\}$ rather than the running graph’s $\{4,3,2,2,1\}$. Different degree multisets forbid an isomorphism. This counterexample concerns the two recovered counts, not equality of the complete characteristic polynomial: the additional coefficients impose further constraints. Later chapters study full spectral determination and cospectrality.

  6. Tree counts with multiple zero modes. Let a graph be two disjoint triangles. Give its Laplacian spectrum and apply the product formula. Why is multiplying only its nonzero eigenvalues and dividing by six incorrect?

    Solution

    A triangle's Laplacian is $3I-J$ and has eigenvalues0,3,3: the constant vector is annihilated and the two-dimensional zero-sum subspace is multiplied by3. The disjoint union is block diagonal, giving spectrum0,0,3,3,3,3. The theorem multiplies all entries except one designated zero, so another zero remains and the tree count is0. Multiplying only the four nonzero entries would give81/6, which is not even an integer and does not count spanning trees. The graph is disconnected, so no spanning tree can exist.

  7. Equality at an extreme versus an interior point. For $D=\operatorname{diag}(3,3,-2)$ describe all nonzero maximizing Rayleigh vectors. Then check whether $x=(1,0,1)^T$ is an eigenvector of $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,1,0)$ even though its quotient is1.

    Solution

    For $y=(a,b,c)^T$, $R_D(y)=(3a^2+3b^2-2c^2)/(a^2+b^2+c^2)$. The largest possible value3 is attained exactly when $c=0$ and $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$. These vectors fill a two-dimensional eigenspace with zero removed. For the second matrix, $x^TMx=2$ and $x^Tx=2$, so the quotient is1. But $Mx=(2,0,0)^T\ne x$, so equality with an interior eigenvalue does not imply an eigen-equation. The extreme deficit proof works because all its coefficients have one sign.

  8. Square inverses need their dimensions. If square matrices $X,Y$ of the same size satisfy $XY=I$, prove $YX=I$. Give rectangular matrices with $XY=I$ but $YX$ not an identity.

    Solution

    The map $X$ is surjective because every vector $z$ is $X(Yz)$. In a finite-dimensional space of the same domain and codomain dimension, rank–nullity makes $X$ injective and therefore invertible. Multiplying $XY=I$ by $X^{-1}$ yields $Y=X^{-1}$, hence $YX=I$. For a rectangular counterexample take $X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $Y=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$. Their product $XY$ is the $1\times1$ identity, but $YX=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ is not $I_2$. The square assumption justified replacing an orthogonal inverse by its transpose in the theorem.

  9. A residual has a bounded interpretation. For a real symmetric $M$, let a unit vector $x$ and real number $\mu$ have $\|Mx-\mu x\|\le10^{-6}$. What can be concluded about the spectrum? Does this prove that $x$ is close to one specified unit eigenvector?

    Solution

    The residual expansion proves that some eigenvalue lies within $10^{-6}$ of $\mu$. It does not identify its index without more information. Nor does it force proximity to one chosen eigenvector: take $M=I_2$, $\mu=1$ and any unit vector. Every residual is zero, while a chosen vector may be perpendicular to $x$. For clustered but distinct eigenvalues, small residuals can also fail to isolate a direction without a gap estimate. Additionally, the conclusion presumes the residual norm bound is established for the exact mathematical inputs; a floating evaluation must account for rounding if it is to serve as a rigorous certificate.

4.13 What spectral coordinates have made possible

A real symmetric matrix acts independently along an orthonormal set of directions. That statement makes walk powers, trace counts, cofactor products and extremal quadratic forms parts of one framework. The proof required a real eigenvector existence argument and invariance of its orthogonal complement; neither a characteristic root count nor orthogonality of a few distinct eigenvectors would have supplied a complete basis by itself.

Graph spectra compress information. Total closed-walk counts follow from eigenvalues, whereas local walk counts require eigenvector coordinates. Repeated eigenvalues permit different bases of the same space, and general nonsymmetric matrices may lack an eigenbasis altogether. The exact code validates one finite construction; approximate reconstruction has a declared tolerance and does not certify huge integer counts. The next chapter will use powers and annihilating polynomials to connect spectral diversity with graph diameter and rank.
