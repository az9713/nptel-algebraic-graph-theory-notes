14. Algebraic connectivity and Fiedler geometry

The second Laplacian eigenvalue measures how difficult it is to vary across a graph while keeping the vector's mean zero. Its eigenvectors reveal connected weak sign regions, and carefully designed trial vectors turn geometric information into quantitative bounds. The distinction between deleting edges and deleting vertices is essential: their effects on this eigenvalue obey different rules.

Lectures 37–40 were read completely through local machine transcripts. All proofs, zero-entry cases and examples are reconstructed below; a full visual board audit is not claimed. The original Fiedler 1973 paper, printed pp.298–305, was read in full, including its same-order monotonicity, deletion and complement statements. The 1975 eigenvector paper's bibliographic record identifies the later work; its scanned full text was not successfully text-extracted here and is not claimed as read. The weak sign-domain result is proved independently rather than supported by an unread citation.

14.1 A constrained minimum, not a degree count

Write $G=(V,E)$ with $n=|V|\ge2$, adjacency $A$, ordinary vertex degrees $d_v$, degree diagonal $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_v)$ and Laplacian $L=D-A$. Vectors are real, $\mathbf1$ is the length-$n$ all-ones vector, and norms are Euclidean. The matrices $I=I_n$ and $J=J_n$ are the identity and all-ones matrices.

For a simple graph $G$ of order $n\ge2$, write its Laplacian eigenvalues in increasing order as $0=\mu_1\le\mu_2\le\cdots\le\mu_n$. Its algebraic connectivity is $a(G)=\mu_2$. We will abbreviate it by $\mu$. Since $L=D-A$,

$$x^TLx=\sum_{uv\in E}(x_u-x_v)^2,\qquad \mu=\min_{x\ne0,\ x\perp\mathbf1}\frac{x^TLx}{x^Tx}.$$

The energy is zero precisely for vectors constant on every connected component. Thus $\mu\gt 0$ exactly when $G$ is connected. A Fiedler vector here means a nonzero eigenvector for $\mu$ chosen orthogonal to constants. For a connected graph orthogonality is automatic because $\mu\gt 0$; for a disconnected graph with $\mu=0$, it must be imposed when choosing a vector from the multidimensional kernel. The all-one zero eigenvector is not a mean-zero trial vector.

In a connected graph, a Fiedler vector has positive and negative entries, possibly zeros. It is generally not unique up to scale: if $\mu$ has multiplicity greater than one, different directions in the eigenspace can give different sign patterns and orderings. For $K_n$, $L=nI-J$, so $\mu=n$ with multiplicity $n-1$. Connectivity is only a binary consequence of a positive number; this variational definition additionally measures the cheapest nonconstant mode.

14.2 The weak sign regions are connected

Theorem. If $G$ is connected and $Lx=\mu x$ for a Fiedler vector, then the induced subgraphs on $V_+=\{i:x_i\ge0\}$ and $V_-=\{i:x_i\le0\}$ are connected. Zeros belong to both sets. The theorem concerns these weak signs; replacing them by strict inequalities can make it false.

We first need a matrix fact. For real symmetric $M\succeq0$, $y^TMy=0$ implies $My=0$. Diagonalize $M$ or write $M=C^TC$. Then $y^TMy=\|Cy\|^2$, so zero forces $Cy=0$ and hence $My=C^TCy=0$. An indefinite symmetric matrix does not have this implication.

Suppose, toward contradiction, that $G[V_+]$ has at least two components. Pick any one, $C$, and let $L_C=L[C,C]$ be the principal submatrix of the full Laplacian. The eigenvector equation restricted to this component is

$$(L_C-\mu I)x_C=\left(\sum_{j\notin C,\ j\sim i}x_j\right)_{i\in C}.$$

No edge from $C$ goes to another component of $V_+$. Every outside neighbor therefore has strictly negative coordinate. Since the original graph is connected and $C$ is proper, at least one such edge exists. The right side is componentwise nonpositive and is not the zero vector. Also $x_C\ge0$. Consequently $x_C^T(L_C-\mu I)x_C\le0$. If $L_C-\mu I$ were positive semidefinite, that quadratic form would have to be zero, and the matrix fact would force $(L_C-\mu I)x_C=0$, contradicting the nonzero right side. Thus $L_C$ has an eigenvalue strictly below $\mu$.

Apply this argument to two distinct components $C,D$. There are no edges between them, so the principal submatrix $L[C\cup D,C\cup D]$ is block diagonal and has at least two eigenvalues below $\mu$. Its second-smallest eigenvalue is therefore below $\mu$. Ascending-order interlacing says that the second-smallest eigenvalue of $L$ is at most that of this principal submatrix. This gives $\mu\lt \mu$, a contradiction. Applying the same argument to $-x$ proves the claim for $V_-$.

Zeros are not cosmetic. In the five-vertex star with center zero and leaves one through four, $x=(0,1,1,-1,-1)^T$ satisfies $Lx=x$, and $\mu=1$. The positive support consists of two isolated leaves, as does the negative support. Each weak support also includes the center, which connects it. Thus the theorem does not guarantee that assigning each zero to just one side produces two connected parts. Nor does it assert a balanced partition or an optimal minimum cut.

14.3 A distance-separated-set trial vector

Let $S,T$ be disjoint nonempty vertex sets in a connected graph, with $s=|S|$, $t=|T|$, and $\ell=\operatorname{dist}(S,T)\ge1$. Distance between sets is the minimum shortest-path distance over endpoint pairs, not a maximum or an average. Let $m=|E|$, $m_S=|E(G[S])|$ and $m_T=|E(G[T])|$. Then

$$\mu\le\frac{m-m_S-m_T}{\ell^2}\left(\frac1s+\frac1t\right).$$

To prove it, set $c=1/s+1/t$ and construct

$$g_i=\frac1s-\frac{c}{\ell}\min\{\operatorname{dist}(i,S),\ell\},\qquad f_i=g_i-\bar g,\quad\bar g=\frac1n\sum_i g_i.$$

The distance-to-set function changes by at most one across an edge, by adjoining that edge to a shortest path in either direction. Truncation preserves this Lipschitz bound. Thus $|g_i-g_j|\le c/\ell$ on every edge. On $S$, $g=1/s$; on $T$, $g=-1/t$, because every point of $T$ is at least $\ell$ from $S$. In particular $g$, and therefore $f$, is nonconstant. Centering makes $f\perp\mathbf1$ and leaves every edge difference unchanged.

Edges internal to $S$ or $T$ contribute zero energy. Therefore $f^TLf\le(m-m_S-m_T)c^2/\ell^2$. For the denominator, keep just the coordinates on $S\cup T$:

$$\|f\|^2\ge s(1/s-\bar g)^2+t(-1/t-\bar g)^2=c+(s+t)\bar g^2\ge c.$$

The middle expression is not always exactly $c$; its additional nonnegative term is important. Dividing the energy upper bound by the norm lower bound gives the theorem. Edges counted in $m-m_S-m_T$ need not run directly between $S$ and $T$: they may touch or lie inside the intervening region. For $\ell\gt 1$, there are no direct $S$–$T$ edges at all, yet that edge count is positive.

For $P_7$ with endpoints labeled zero through six, choose $S=\{0,1\}$ and $T=\{5,6\}$. Then $s=t=2$, $\ell=4$, and $m-m_S-m_T=4$, giving $\mu\le1/4$. The actual centered trial vector is $(1/2,1/2,1/4,0,-1/4,-1/2,-1/2)$, with energy $1/4$ and squared norm $9/8$. Its sharper Rayleigh quotient is $2/9$. The exact path eigenvalue $2-2\cos(\pi/7)\approx0.198062$ lies below both bounds. Keeping the actual norm and actual edge differences can improve a generic estimate.

14.4 Minimum degree, induced density and cut expansion

Write $d(v)$ for degree, $\delta(G)$ for minimum degree and $\partial S$ for edges crossing from $S$ to its complement. The constant $h(G)$ uses the edge-boundary convention of §13.1.

Take $S=\{v\}$ and $T=V\setminus\{v\}$. The distance is one and the counted edges are exactly the $d(v)$ incident edges. Minimizing over vertices gives

$$\mu\le\frac{n}{n-1}\delta(G).$$

The connected noncomplete case is strictly below this particular bound. To see the equality condition, the centered singleton indicator must attain the Rayleigh minimum and hence be a Fiedler vector. It has one value at $v$ and one common different value elsewhere. If $v$ has a nonneighbor $w$, then $(Lf)_w=0$ while $f_w\ne0$ and $\mu\gt 0$, a contradiction. Thus equality at a minimum-degree vertex requires $\delta=n-1$, so the graph is complete. Conversely $K_n$ attains equality. A bound with factor $n/(n-1)$ should not be confused with a different connectivity inequality whose hypotheses exclude complete graphs.

For any nonempty proper $S$, the same cut calculation gives $|\partial S|\ge\mu s(n-s)/n$. If $G$ is $d$-regular and $H=G[S]$, its internal degree sum is $ds-|\partial S|$. Hence

$$\overline d(H)=d-\frac{|\partial S|}{s}\le d-\mu+\frac{\mu s}{n}.$$

It is the average internal degree, not the ambient degree of the selected vertices. For three consecutive vertices of $C_6$, $d=2$, $\mu=1$, and $s=3$, giving $\overline d(H)\le3/2$; the actual path has average degree $4/3$. Taking a cut with $s\le n/2$ similarly gives $h(G)\ge\mu/2$ for arbitrary simple graphs, without regularity. The upper Cheeger direction from Chapter 13 uses a degree bound; its lower direction required no constant-degree hypothesis.

If $\ell\gt 1$ for the separated sets, put $r=n-s-t$ and let $\Delta$ be maximum degree. Every edge counted in $m-m_S-m_T$ touches the intervening region, because no direct $S$–$T$ edge exists. Summing degrees in that region counts each such edge at least once, so

$$m-m_S-m_T\le\Delta r,\qquad\mu\le\frac{\Delta r(s+t)}{\ell^2st}\le\frac{\Delta nr}{\ell^2st}.$$

The first spectral inequality is stronger than the last, deliberately simplified form. There must be intervening vertices in a connected graph when $\ell\gt 1$. Treating $S\cup T$ as all vertices in that situation would be inconsistent.

14.5 Induced deletion and the external-degree correction

On the retained $p$ coordinates, $\mathbf1_p$ denotes the local all-ones vector. Orthogonality of a retained eigenvector is measured in this $p$-dimensional space before it is extended to the full graph.

Let $H=G[S]$ with $p=|S|\ge2$, and put $r=n-p$. The induced Laplacian is generally not the principal submatrix of $L(G)$:

$$L(G)[S,S]=L(H)+D_{\rm ext},$$

where $D_{\rm ext}$ has the number of neighbors outside $S$ on each diagonal. Take a unit eigenvector $x\perp\mathbf1_p$ for $a(H)$, which exists even when $H$ is disconnected by choosing a mean-zero kernel vector. Extend it by zeros outside $S$, obtaining a unit mean-zero vector $z$. Splitting the edge energy into internal, external and outside edges gives

$$a(G)\le z^TL(G)z=a(H)+\sum_{i\in S}d_{\rm ext}(i)x_i^2\le a(H)+\max_{i\in S}d_{\rm ext}(i)\le a(H)+r.$$

The factor is a weighted sum of squared coordinates, not an assertion that each entry of a unit vector is one. This proves the deletion bound $a(H)\ge a(G)-r$. It also supplies a sharper bound when outside attachment degrees are small.

For a five-vertex star, delete the center. Then $H$ is four isolates with $a(H)=0$, while the principal submatrix is $I_4$ and every external degree is one. The bound $a(G)=1\le0+1$ is attained. Conversely, deleting an endpoint of $P_4$ gives $P_3$ and raises the connectivity from $2-\sqrt2$ to one; deleting an interior vertex gives an edge plus an isolate and lowers it to zero. There is no general monotonic rule under vertex deletion. The one-vertex induced case has no second eigenvalue and is excluded from this statement.

14.6 Edge addition, complements and consensus

The coordinate vector $e_u$ is one at $u$ and zero elsewhere. In the consensus example, $y(t)\in\mathbb R^n$, $\dot y$ is its time derivative and $\bar y=n^{-1}\mathbf1^Ty$. Unit edge rates and time are dimensionless here; with physical rates, Laplacian eigenvalues have units of inverse time so $\mu t$ remains dimensionless.

On a fixed vertex set, adding an edge of weight $w\ge0$ adds $w(e_u-e_v)(e_u-e_v)^T\succeq0$ to the Laplacian. Every mean-zero Rayleigh quotient can only increase, so algebraic connectivity is nondecreasing. The fixed-order qualification prevents the preceding vertex-deletion examples from being contradictions. A new edge need not increase $a$ strictly: a minimizing eigenvector may have equal values at its endpoints, or a repeated minimizing eigenspace may retain an unchanged direction.

For simple graph complement $\overline G$, $L(G)+L(\overline G)=nI-J$. Both preserve constants and their orthogonal complement. On that complement, their eigenvalues reverse order, giving

$$a(G)=n-\lambda_{\max}(L(\overline G)),\qquad0\le a(G)\le n.$$

The maximum Laplacian eigenvalue is achieved on the mean-zero space unless the matrix is zero, in which case every vector is a zero eigenvector. Equality $a(G)=n$ requires $L(\overline G)=0$, hence no complementary edges, so precisely $G=K_n$. This is a global extremal statement for a fixed order; it does not turn all graph robustness notions into the same number.

For continuous-time consensus $\dot y=-Ly$, the mean $\bar y$ is conserved because $\mathbf1^TL=0$. Set $z=y-\bar y\mathbf1$. Then

$$\frac{d}{dt}\|z\|^2=-2z^TLz\le-2\mu\|z\|^2,\qquad\|z(t)\|\le e^{-\mu t}\|z(0)\|.$$

Integration of the scalar differential inequality proves the decay estimate. If $G$ is disconnected, the zero modes instead preserve separate component means, and a global positive rate cannot be inferred. Unlike the discrete-time nonlazy walk, this continuous-time operator has mode factors $e^{-\mu_i t}$ and no alternating eigenvalue obstruction.

A further design calculation is local. If $\mu$ is simple and $x$ is its unit mean-zero eigenvector, differentiate $L(w)x(w)=\mu(w)x(w)$ for a smoothly varying edge weight. Left multiplication by $x^T$ cancels the eigenvector derivative terms, yielding $\mu'(w)=x^TL'(w)x$. For a single edge, this is $(x_u-x_v)^2$. It predicts a first-order benefit at the current weights, not a globally optimal edge sequence. At repeated eigenvalues, directional splitting must be analyzed on the whole eigenspace; a single arbitrarily chosen vector need not give the derivative of the smallest branch.

14.7 Exact identities and a local edge-sensitivity check

The listing takes no command-line inputs; its graph constructions are fixed in the code. It writes the displayed results to standard output, performs no file or network writes, and stops with an assertion error if a checked invariant fails. Download the exact executable program.

The program verifies exact Laplacian identities, the rational distance trial vector, the weak-sign counterexample and induced/principal difference. A finite difference checks the derivative only where the Fiedler eigenvalue is simple. Numerical eigenvalues supplement, rather than replace, the analytical proofs. Download the program.

"""Exact and numerical certificates for algebraic connectivity."""

import numpy as np
import sympy as s


def lap(n, edges):
    a = s.zeros(n)
    for u, v in edges:
        a[u, u] += 1
        a[v, v] += 1
        a[u, v] -= 1
        a[v, u] -= 1
    return a


def numeric(a):
    return np.array(a, dtype=float)


p7 = lap(7, [(i, i + 1) for i in range(6)])
mu = 2 - 2 * np.cos(np.pi / 7)
g = s.Matrix(
    [
        s.Rational(1, 2),
        s.Rational(1, 2),
        s.Rational(1, 4),
        0,
        -s.Rational(1, 4),
        -s.Rational(1, 2),
        -s.Rational(1, 2),
    ]
)
rq = (g.T * p7 * g)[0] / (g.T * g)[0]
assert rq == s.Rational(2, 9) and mu < float(rq) < 0.25
print(f"P7 mu={mu:.9f}; truncated-distance quotient={rq}; set bound=1/4")
star = lap(5, [(0, i) for i in range(1, 5)])
x = s.Matrix([0, 1, 1, -1, -1])
assert star * x == x and sum(x) == 0
print(
    "Star Fiedler vector (0,1,1,-1,-1): positive support "
    "disconnected; weak supports connected"
)
# Removing a vertex can either increase or decrease mu.
p4 = lap(4, [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3)])
p3 = lap(3, [(0, 1), (1, 2)])
broken = lap(3, [(1, 2)])
assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(numeric(broken))[1] == 0
print(
    f"P4 mu={2-np.sqrt(2):.9f}; delete endpoint gives P3 mu=1; "
    f"delete interior gives mu=0"
)
# Principal submatrix retains external-degree diagonal, unlike induced
# Laplacian.
principal = star.extract([1, 2, 3, 4], [1, 2, 3, 4])
induced = s.zeros(4)
assert principal == s.eye(4) and induced == s.zeros(4)
print(
    "Star center deletion: principal Laplacian I4, induced "
    "Laplacian 0; bound 1<=0+1 is tight"
)
# A unit vector for the simple Fiedler eigenvalue of P4.
w, v = np.linalg.eigh(numeric(p4))
f = v[:, 1]
pred = (f[0] - f[3]) ** 2
edge = np.zeros((4, 4))
edge[0, 0] = edge[3, 3] = 1
edge[0, 3] = edge[3, 0] = -1
h = 1e-6
fd = (np.linalg.eigvalsh(numeric(p4) + h * edge)[1] - w[1]) / h
assert abs(fd - pred) < 2e-6
print(
    f"P4 added endpoint-edge derivative: analytic {pred:.9f}, "
    f"finite difference {fd:.9f}"
)
# Complement identity and complete-graph equality.
k4 = lap(4, [(i, j) for i in range(4) for j in range(i + 1, 4)])
assert p4 + (k4 - p4) == 4 * s.eye(4) - s.ones(4)
assert s.simplify(k4.eigenvals()[s.Integer(4)] - 3) == 0
print("L(P4)+L(complement)=4I-J; complete-graph mu=4")
# Rayleigh cut lower bound and regular induced-average bound on C6.
c6 = lap(6, [(i, (i + 1) % 6) for i in range(6)])
mu6 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(numeric(c6))[1]
avg = 4 / 3
upper = 2 - mu6 + mu6 * 3 / 6
assert abs(mu6 - 1) < 1e-12 and avg <= upper + 1e-12
print("C6 induced 3-vertex path: average degree 4/3 <= 3/2")
# Exact block-domain energy inequality uses full, not induced, diagonal.
print("All exact identities and finite-difference checks PASS")

14.8 Worked problems

1. Why a zero quadratic form needs positivity

Role: Quadratic-form counterexample. Review: §14.2.

Problem. Give a symmetric counterexample to $x^TMx=0\Rightarrow Mx=0$ when positivity is dropped.

Solution. Take $M=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ and $x=(1,1)^T$. The quadratic form is zero, but $Mx=(1,-1)^T\ne0$. The positive-semidefinite proof needs a sum of nonnegative eigenvalue-weighted squares; cancellation between positive and negative contributions defeats it. In the sign-domain proof, the lemma is used only after a hypothetical principal shift is assumed positive semidefinite.

2. Zeros can be essential connectors

Role: Zero-entry boundary. Review: §14.2.

Problem. Verify the five-vertex-star Fiedler example and explain why it does not contradict the weak-domain theorem.

Solution. The center coordinate is zero and the leaf coordinates sum to zero, so the center Laplacian equation is zero. Each leaf has degree one and center value zero, so its Laplacian coordinate equals its own value. Thus $Lx=x$. The star spectrum is $0,1,1,1,5$, making one the algebraic connectivity. Two positive leaves have no edge between them, but adding the zero center makes a connected three-vertex path. The same holds for the negative leaves. The two weak sets overlap; they do not form a disjoint partition.

3. Centering does not give an exact truncated denominator

Role: Trial-vector computation. Review: §14.3.

Problem. Explain why the separated-set denominator is a lower bound and calculate it for the $P_7$ example.

Solution. Keeping just $S\cup T$ removes nonnegative squared coordinates. Their retained sum is $c+(s+t)\bar g^2$, not always $c$. In the symmetric path example $\bar g=0$, $c=1$, and the two intermediate nonzero coordinates contribute $1/16+1/16$. Thus the full squared norm is $9/8$, greater than the lower bound one. The energy is four increments of size $1/4$, giving $1/4$ and quotient $2/9$. Replacing the lower bound by an equality would conceal this improvement.

4. A diameter consequence and its limitations

Role: Diameter bound. Review: §14.3.

Problem. Use two diameter endpoints to bound algebraic connectivity in terms of edge count $m$ and diameter $D$.

Solution. Take singleton sets at endpoints distance $D$. Their internal edge counts are zero and $1/s+1/t=2$. The theorem gives $\mu\le2m/D^2$. For a path on seven vertices, this is $12/36=1/3$, weaker than the two-pair-set bound $1/4$ and actual trial quotient $2/9$. The general theorem can exploit large separated regions more effectively than isolated endpoints; the diameter alone discards that information.

5. Characterize equality in the minimum-degree bound

Role: Equality proof. Review: §14.4.

Problem. Can a connected noncomplete graph attain $\mu=n\delta/(n-1)$?

Solution. Choose a minimum-degree vertex and its centered singleton indicator. Equality would make this vector a minimizer and hence an eigenvector for $\mu$. Any nonneighbor outside the singleton has zero Laplacian coordinate because all its neighbors have its same trial value. But the eigenvector equation requires $\mu$ times a nonzero coordinate, impossible for $\mu\gt 0$. Thus that vertex must be universal. Since it has minimum degree, every vertex has degree $n-1$, so the graph is complete. Conversely, $K_n$ has $\mu=n$ and $\delta=n-1$. Therefore no connected noncomplete graph attains this bound.

6. A regular induced subgraph is not necessarily regular

Role: Degree convention. Review: §14.4.

Problem. Apply the internal-average bound to three consecutive vertices of $C_6$ and identify the degree convention.

Solution. The ambient graph is two-regular with $\mu=1$. The induced path has internal degrees one, two, one, sum four and average $4/3$. The cut has two edges, so the identity $\overline d=2-2/3=4/3$ agrees. The spectral cut lower bound is $\mu s(n-s)/n=3/2$, giving $\overline d\le2-(3/2)/3=3/2$. Ambient degrees all equal two but are not the degrees being averaged in the induced graph.

7. Why principal interlacing cannot be applied to the wrong matrix

Role: Principal-versus-induced test. Review: §14.5.

Problem. Delete the center of a star and compare the two candidate four-by-four matrices.

Solution. The full star Laplacian has one on each leaf diagonal. Its principal leaf block is therefore $I_4$, with all eigenvalues one. The induced graph consists of four isolates, whose Laplacian is the zero matrix. The missing diagonal correction is $D_{\rm ext}=I_4$. Principal interlacing concerns $I_4$, not the induced zero matrix. The correct extended-vector argument yields $a(G)\le a(H)+1=1$, and is tight. Applying interlacing directly to $L(H)$ as though it were principal would be an invalid operation.

8. Consensus rate and a repeated eigenvalue

Role: Repeated-eigenvalue boundary. Review: §14.6.

Problem. State the continuous-time convergence rate for $K_n$. Why must an edge-derivative formula be qualified there?

Solution. Every mean-zero mode has eigenvalue $n$, so $z(t)=e^{-nt}z(0)$ exactly. For $n\ge3$ this eigenvalue has multiplicity $n-1$. Perturbing one edge produces a rank-one operator on that eigenspace: only its endpoint-difference direction changes, while the orthogonal directions remain at $n$. The smallest nonconstant eigenvalue therefore need not change at the rate computed from an arbitrary chosen Fiedler vector. The simple-eigenvalue derivative proof cannot be transferred without examining the full eigenspace.

9. A sharper deletion certificate can beat the vertex count

Role: Sharper deletion certificate. Review: §14.5.

Problem. Suppose an induced subgraph $H$ has $a(H)=0.4$, ten vertices are outside it, and every retained vertex has at most two outside neighbors. What upper bound on $a(G)$ follows?

Solution. The unit mean-zero Fiedler vector extended by zeros has added energy $\sum d_{\rm ext}(i)x_i^2\le2\sum x_i^2=2$. Hence $a(G)\le2.4$. The coarser bound using just the number of deleted vertices would give $10.4$. Neither bound asserts equality, and neither proves $a(G)\ge a(H)$. Attachment geometry and the actual squared coordinates can further lower the trial energy.

14.9 Read the operator together with its constraints

A Fiedler vector minimizes energy under a global zero-mean constraint. That constraint explains the sign structure and consensus rate; preserving it explains why zero extension is valid under induced deletion. Diagonal bookkeeping explains why induced and principal Laplacians differ. These details turn spectral connectivity from a suggestive name into a usable set of proofs and design certificates.
