15. Balance, switching and signed adjacency spectra

A negative edge changes the sign of a walk contribution, but a vertex sign change can relocate many negative edges without changing any cycle sign. Balance asks whether all negative edges can be removed by such coordinate changes. The answer has equivalent descriptions through vertex potentials, cycle products, an exact linear-time consistency test and the largest adjacency eigenvalue. The last characterization needs connectivity and the correct meaning of “largest.”

15.1 Products around cycles

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a finite simple undirected graph and $\sigma:E\to\{-1,1\}$ a signature. The signed adjacency matrix $A_\sigma$ has entry $\sigma(uv)$ on an edge and zero otherwise. Its entrywise absolute value is the unsigned adjacency $A$. There are no loops or parallel edges in this chapter; those would require additional cycle and incidence conventions.

The sign of a walk is the product of signs along its traversed edges, including repeated traversals. The empty walk has product one. Reversing an edge does not change its sign, and traversing it twice gives product one. A cycle is positive when its sign is one, equivalently when it has an even number of negative edges. The signed graph is balanced if every simple cycle is positive. A forest is consequently balanced for every signature, and disconnected balance means balance in every component.

For a triangle, zero or two negative edges give positive product; one or three give negative product. This condition is algebraic. It does not say that an all-negative triangle cannot persist in an empirical network. Nor does checking triangles alone establish balance in an arbitrary sparse graph: a four-cycle with one negative edge and no diagonals has no triangles, but is unbalanced.

Every closed walk in a balanced signed graph is positive. A direct proof uses induction on walk length. A consecutive traversal followed immediately by its reverse can be removed without changing sign. If a remaining closed walk repeats another vertex internally, split it there into shorter closed walks. Continue until the factors are simple cycles and two-edge backtracks, each positive. This also supplies a converse: a negative closed walk contains a negative simple cycle among its factors.

15.2 Balance is exactly a vertex-potential consistency condition

Breadth-first traversal uses a queue to visit vertices by increasing tree distance; depth-first traversal follows an unvisited branch using a stack before returning. The notation $O(|V|+|E|)$ means a constant multiple of vertices plus edges bounds the operation count, independently of graph size.

Balance theorem. The signature is balanced if and only if there is a map $\eta:V\to\{-1,1\}$ such that $\sigma(uv)=\eta_u\eta_v$ on every edge. Equivalently, vertices can be divided into two classes with positive edges inside classes and negative edges between them. Either class may be empty, and no missing edge is being asserted to exist.

If the potentials exist, multiplying along a cycle makes every vertex potential occur twice, so the product is one. Conversely, choose a root in each component and assign it potential one. For a vertex $v$, choose a root-to-$v$ path and let $\eta_v$ be its sign. Two such paths, one followed by the reverse of the other, make a closed walk; its positive sign proves the assigned value is independent of the choice. For an edge $uv$, the root path to $u$, that edge, and the reverse root path from $v$ make another closed walk. Thus $\eta_u\sigma(uv)\eta_v=1$, which gives the edge equation. Isolated vertices can receive either potential.

This proof covers the all-positive graph, which need not contain a negative edge from which to start a construction. It also covers components independently. In a connected graph, two valid potential assignments differ by a global sign: along every edge their ratios agree, and connectivity propagates that equality. With $c$ components, there are exactly $2^c$ valid assignments for a balanced fixed signature, one independent overall flip per component.

The condition is easily certified without enumerating cycles. Run a breadth-first or depth-first spanning-forest traversal. On a tree edge from an assigned $u$ to an unassigned $v$, set $\eta_v=\sigma(uv)\eta_u$. Then check every remaining edge against $\sigma(uv)=\eta_u\eta_v$. If all pass, the potentials certify balance. A failing edge plus the unique tree path between its endpoints is an explicit negative cycle. Each vertex and edge is processed a constant number of times, yielding $O(|V|+|E|)$ work for adjacency lists. This computational claim is about balance, not finding a minimum number of deletions to repair imbalance.

15.3 Switching is a change of coordinates

The operator $\operatorname{diag}(\eta)$ places the vertex signs on a diagonal. A symmetric difference of two sets contains elements belonging to exactly one of them.

For any $\eta:V\to\{-1,1\}$, define $\sigma^\eta(uv)=\eta_u\sigma(uv)\eta_v$. If $U=\{v:\eta_v=-1\}$, this reverses precisely the signs on edges crossing the cut $(U,V\setminus U)$. Edges inside either side retain their signs. Switching by the whole component does nothing, and successive switchings multiply their vertex potentials, equivalently combine switched sets by symmetric difference.

With $D_\eta=\operatorname{diag}(\eta)$,

$$A_{\sigma^\eta}=D_\eta A_\sigma D_\eta=D_\eta^{-1}A_\sigma D_\eta.$$

The matrix is an orthogonal conjugate, so every eigenvalue and multiplicity is preserved; an eigenvector $x$ becomes $D_\eta x$. This is not necessarily an isomorphism preserving edge signs. It preserves the underlying graph but changes its signature. Chapter11's Seidel switching toggled adjacency across a cut in an associated representation; signed switching here changes the signs of existing edges and leaves nonedges absent.

Every closed-walk sign is unchanged, since vertex factors occur in pairs. Thus balance is switching invariant. The balance theorem now says that a signed graph is balanced exactly when it can be switched to the all-positive signature of its underlying graph. Negative edge count itself is not invariant: a balanced signed tree can have many negative edges before switching and none afterward.

A useful normal form uses a chosen spanning forest. Assign potentials by its path signs, so every tree edge becomes positive. Each non-tree edge then has sign equal to the sign of its fundamental cycle. It follows that two signatures on the same labeled underlying graph are switching equivalent exactly when their cycle signs agree. For the converse, normalize both on the same forest: their tree signs are positive and their chord signs agree, hence their normalized signatures coincide.

15.4 A binary linear-algebra view and a count of switching classes

Put $n=|V|$, $m=|E|$ and let $c$ be the number of connected components. The field $\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}$ uses addition and multiplication modulo two.

Encode edge signs by bits $b_e\in\mathbb F_2$, with $\sigma(e)=(-1)^{b_e}$. Encode vertex potentials similarly by $t_v$. Switching changes

$$b_{uv}\longmapsto b_{uv}+t_u+t_v\pmod2.$$

Thus the changes are cut vectors, and the parity around a cycle is unchanged because each vertex is counted an even number of times. Fixing a forest removes $n-c$ independent edge bits and leaves $m-n+c$ chord bits. Each chord bit can be chosen freely and records its fundamental-cycle product. There are therefore exactly $2^{m-n+c}$ switching classes of signatures on a fixed labeled graph with $n$ vertices, $m$ edges and $c$ components.

There are $2^n$ vertex sign choices, but the kernel of their action has $2^c$ elements: a potential assignment acts trivially precisely when it is constant on every component. Each switching class thus has $2^{n-c}$ signatures. Multiplying the class count by the class size recovers all $2^m$ signatures. The single balanced class is the class of the all-positive signature. Isolates contribute to both $n$ and $c$, and correctly do not introduce edge-sign choices.

This exact classification keeps vertex labels fixed. If vertex permutations are also allowed, several such classes can merge. Equal adjacency spectra can also occur in distinct fixed-label switching classes. For $K_4$, put the sole negative edge at01 in one signature and at02 in another. Permuting labels gives the same spectrum, but the triangle013 is negative in the first and positive in the second, so no fixed-label switching relates them. A spectral equality is weaker than equality of every labeled cycle invariant.

15.5 Full spectral equality detects balance, even without connectivity

The walk length $k$ is a nonnegative integer. A rooted closed walk has its starting vertex marked; opposite traversals are different walks. In characteristic polynomials below, $x$ is a scalar variable.

Matrix multiplication gives a signed walk expansion:

$$(A_\sigma^k)_{uv}=\sum_{W:u\to v,\ |W|=k}\sigma(W).$$

The proof is induction, appending one signed edge to each shorter walk. Taking traces sums all rooted closed walks. If $N_k^-$ counts the negative rooted closed walks of length $k$ in the underlying graph, then

$$\operatorname{tr}(A^k)-\operatorname{tr}(A_\sigma^k)=2N_k^-\ge0.$$

The unsigned trace counts each walk as one; the signed trace counts positive walks as one and negative walks as minus one. Their difference has no cancellation. If $A$ and $A_\sigma$ have the same spectrum, all their power traces agree, so there are no negative closed walks and hence no negative cycles. The signature is balanced. Conversely balance gives $A_\sigma=D_\eta A D_\eta$ and therefore the full same spectrum. This proof works componentwise without assuming connectivity.

In fact, checking traces for $1\le k\le n$ is sufficient for balance: any negative simple cycle has length at most $n$ and produces negative rooted walks at that length. If the shortest negative cycle length is $r$, then all deficits below $r$ vanish, and at length $r$ every negative closed walk must itself be a simple negative cycle. There are two traversal directions and $r$ choices of starting vertex per such cycle. Therefore the first nonzero deficit equals $4r$ times the number of negative cycles of length $r$.

For $K_4$ with exactly one negative edge, precisely two triangles contain that edge. The third-trace deficit is $4\cdot3\cdot2=24$. The exact signed adjacency polynomial is $(x-1)(x+1)(x^2-5)$, while the all-positive polynomial is $(x-3)(x+1)^3$. The spectral discrepancy records negative walk contributions; it is not a claim that the number of negative edges alone determines every signed spectrum.

15.6 For a connected support, the largest eigenvalue alone suffices

The sign function is $\operatorname{sgn}(t)=+1$ for $t>0$ and $-1$ for $t<0$. It is applied below only after proving that every eigenvector coordinate is nonzero.

For every real unit vector $x$,

$$x^TA_\sigma x\le |x|^TA|x|\le\lambda_1(A).$$

The first inequality is termwise on edges; the second is the Rayleigh upper bound and $\||x|\|=1$. Maximizing proves $\lambda_1(A_\sigma)\le\lambda_1(A)$. The last inequality is not automatically equality for an arbitrary vector.

If the support graph is connected, equality holds if and only if the signature is balanced. The forward implication for a balanced signature follows from switching. For the converse, take a unit top eigenvector $x$ of $A_\sigma$ and assume the top roots agree. Every inequality in the chain must be equality. Rayleigh equality says $|x|$ is a top eigenvector of $A$. The connected Perron theorem from Chapter6 makes every coordinate of $|x|$ strictly positive, including the trivial one-vertex case treated separately. Consequently no coordinate of $x$ is zero. Edgewise equality now requires

$$\sigma(uv)x_ux_v=|x_u||x_v|,\qquad\sigma(uv)=\operatorname{sgn}(x_u)\operatorname{sgn}(x_v).$$

These vertex signs are the balance potentials. Equivalently, switching the negative coordinates of $x$ makes every edge positive. Merely observing a positive eigenvector of some signed matrix would not establish this result; the equality of the two top roots and the Perron equality conditions do the work.

Connectivity cannot be dropped. Take an all-positive $K_4$ component and an unbalanced signed triangle component. Both the whole signed matrix and its unsigned counterpart have largest eigenvalue three, supplied by the $K_4$. The smaller component can remain unbalanced without changing that maximum. Full spectral equality would still detect it. For disconnected graphs, the top-root test can be applied separately to each connected component.

15.7 Absolute spectral radius detects two different possibilities

For a symmetric matrix, the absolute spectral radius is $\rho(A_\sigma)=\max_i|\lambda_i(A_\sigma)|$, whereas the largest algebraic eigenvalue is $\lambda_1$. Repeating the Rayleigh bound for $-A_\sigma$ yields $\rho(A_\sigma)\le\lambda_1(A)=\rho(A)$. Equality of absolute radii does not alone prove balance.

Call a signature antibalanced if its global negation $-\sigma$ is balanced. Equivalently, $\sigma(C)=(-1)^{|C|}$ on every cycle, or it can be switched to the all-negative signature. On a connected support, equality $\rho(A_\sigma)=\rho(A)$ occurs precisely when either $\lambda_1(A_\sigma)=\rho(A)$ or $\lambda_1(-A_\sigma)=\rho(A)$. The just-proved top-root theorem makes these the balanced and antibalanced cases respectively.

An all-negative triangle is antibalanced and unbalanced. Its eigenvalues are $-2,1,1$, so its largest algebraic eigenvalue is one, below the unsigned top root two, while its absolute radius is two. This is a direct counterexample to replacing “largest eigenvalue” by “absolute radius” in the balance test. A support admits a signature that is both balanced and antibalanced exactly when every cycle has even length, that is, when the support is bipartite. In that case the two classes coincide; on a nonbipartite connected support they are distinct.

For a signed cycle of length $n\ge3$, switching can make all but the closing edge positive. Its remaining sign is the total cycle product. In the negative case put $z_j=e^{i(2j+1)\pi/n}$, $0\le j<n$, where $i^2=-1$. The vector with coordinate $r$ equal to $z_j^r$, $0\le r<n$, obeys the interior recurrence with eigenvalue $z_j+z_j^{-1}=2\cos[(2j+1)\pi/n]$. Its antiperiodic extension satisfies $v_n=-v_0$ and $v_{-1}=-v_{n-1}$ because $z_j^n=-1$; these are exactly the equations at the negative closing edge. For distinct modes the inner product is a geometric sum whose ratio is a nontrivial $n$th root of unity, so it is zero. Each mode has squared norm $n$. The $n$ orthogonal nonzero modes form a complete complex eigenbasis, establishing every multiplicity; the real symmetric spectrum has those same real values. A negative four-cycle has $\pm\sqrt2$, each twice, and radius below two; a negative odd cycle has eigenvalue minus two and is antibalanced. Parity explains the difference.

15.8 Enumerate small switching classes and test the boundary cases

The listing takes no command-line inputs; its graph constructions are fixed in the code. It writes the displayed results to standard output, performs no file or network writes, and stops with an assertion error if a checked invariant fails. Download the exact executable program.

The program checks every one of the64 signatures on labeled $K_4$, normalizes a fixed spanning tree and tests both equality classifications. Exact characteristic polynomials and traces establish the named examples; floating eigensolvers are restricted to well-separated finite equality checks with stated numerical tolerance. Download the program.

"""Switching, cycle invariants and signed spectral equality checks."""

from itertools import combinations, product
import numpy as np
import sympy as s

edges = list(combinations(range(4), 2))
x = s.symbols("x")


def matrix(signs):
    a = s.zeros(4)
    for (u, v), z in zip(edges, signs):
        a[u, v] = a[v, u] = z
    return a


def gauge(signs):
    # Tree edges 01,02,03 are switched positive with root potential +1.
    eta = [1, signs[0], signs[1], signs[2]]
    return tuple(eta[u] * z * eta[v] for (u, v), z in zip(edges, signs))


classes = {}
balanced = anti = 0
for signs in product((-1, 1), repeat=6):
    canonical = gauge(signs)
    classes[canonical] = classes.get(canonical, 0) + 1
    a = matrix(signs)
    d = np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array(a, dtype=float))
    isbal = all(z == 1 for z in canonical)
    isanti = all(z == 1 for z in gauge(tuple(-v for v in signs)))
    balanced += isbal
    anti += isanti
    assert (abs(d[-1] - 3) < 1e-10) == isbal
    assert (abs(max(abs(d)) - 3) < 1e-10) == (isbal or isanti)
assert len(classes) == 8 and set(classes.values()) == {8}
print(
    f"K4 signatures=64: switching classes={len(classes)}, each "
    f"size=8; balanced={balanced}, antibalanced={anti}"
)
print(
    "All K4 signatures: top-eigenvalue and absolute-radius "
    "equality classifications PASS"
)
a = matrix([-1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])
b = matrix([1, -1, 1, 1, 1, 1])
assert a.charpoly(x).as_expr() == b.charpoly(x).as_expr() and gauge(
    [-1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]
) != gauge([1, -1, 1, 1, 1, 1])
print("One-negative-edge K4 polynomial:", s.factor(a.charpoly(x).as_expr()))
print(
    "Equal spectra but different fixed-label cycle signs: not "
    "switching equivalent"
)
unsigned = s.ones(4) - s.eye(4)
assert s.trace(unsigned**3) - s.trace(a**3) == 24
print("K4 third-trace deficit=24=4*3*2 negative triangles")
triangle = s.ones(3) - s.eye(3)
negative = -triangle
assert negative.eigenvals() == {s.Integer(-2): 1, s.Integer(1): 2}
print(
    "All-negative triangle: largest eigenvalue 1, absolute "
    "radius 2; unbalanced but antibalanced"
)
# A positive K4 component hides the smaller unbalanced component from the top
# root.
unbalanced = triangle.copy()
unbalanced[0, 1] = unbalanced[1, 0] = -1
block = s.diag(unsigned, unbalanced)
assert max(block.eigenvals()) == 3
print(
    "Disconnected K4 plus unbalanced triangle: signed/unsigned "
    "top root both 3, whole graph unbalanced"
)
# Test explicit diagonal conjugation and a negative square.
d = s.diag(1, -1, 1, -1)
assert d * a * d == matrix(
    [int(d[u, u] * a[u, v] * d[v, v]) for u, v in edges]
)
c = s.zeros(4)
for u, v, z in [(0, 1, 1), (1, 2, 1), (2, 3, 1), (3, 0, -1)]:
    c[u, v] = c[v, u] = z
assert s.factor(c.charpoly(x).as_expr()) == (x * x - 2) ** 2
print("Unbalanced C4 polynomial=(x^2-2)^2; radius sqrt(2)<2")

15.9 Worked problems

1. A triangle test has a graph-dependent scope

Role: Triangle-test scope. Review: §15.1.

Problem. Why does checking every triangle suffice for a complete graph but fail for an arbitrary graph?

Solution. In a complete graph, split any longer cycle by chords into triangles. Multiplying their signs cancels every interior chord twice, leaving the original cycle product. If every triangle is positive, so is every cycle. In a chordless four-cycle with one negative edge, no triangle exists, so every triangle test passes vacuously while the cycle is negative. A spanning-forest fundamental-cycle test works in both settings.

2. Construct potentials and a negative-cycle certificate

Role: Negative-cycle certificate. Review: §15.2.

Problem. A four-cycle01230 has signs minus, plus, minus, plus in that order. Find balance potentials rooted at0. What changes if edge30 is made negative?

Solution. Set $\eta_0=1$. Along the path0123 the potentials are $\eta_1=-1$, $\eta_2=-1$, $\eta_3=1$. The closing positive edge30 agrees with $\eta_3\eta_0=1$, so the signature is balanced. Changing just that edge to negative violates the equation, and the entire four-cycle now has three negative edges. The failed chord plus its tree path supplies a concrete negative-cycle witness; no global search over all cycles is needed.

3. Count signature classes with isolates present

Role: Class-count calculation. Review: §15.4.

Problem. Take a triangle, a separate single edge and two isolates. Find $n,m,c$, the number of classes and their sizes.

Solution. There are seven vertices, four edges and four components. Thus $m-n+c=1$ and there are two switching classes, distinguished by the triangle sign. Each has $2^{n-c}=2^3=8$ signatures, totaling sixteen. The single edge has no cycle invariant and either sign can be removed by switching. Each isolate contributes a stabilizing vertex sign but no edge-sign choice. Exactly one of the two classes is balanced.

4. Compare switching, relabeling and spectra

Role: Equivalence comparison. Review: §15.3.

Problem. Explain the one-negative-edge $K_4$ examples at01 and02.

Solution. Exchanging labels1 and2 maps one signed graph to the other, giving permutation-similar adjacency matrices and identical polynomials $(x-1)(x+1)(x^2-5)$. However triangle013 contains the negative edge in the first signature and no negative edge in the second. Fixed-label switching preserves that triangle sign, so it cannot connect them. Switching isomorphism, which allows relabeling too, is a different equivalence relation from the fixed-label switching considered in the class count.

5. Recover a negative-cycle count from traces

Role: Trace inverse inference. Review: §15.5.

Problem. If the first nonzero signed-versus-unsigned trace deficit is at length five and equals forty, what does it establish?

Solution. No shorter negative simple cycle exists, because it would already produce a positive earlier deficit. At length five, a negative closed walk cannot contain repeated vertices or backtracks without yielding a shorter negative cycle, so it must traverse a negative five-cycle. Each such cycle contributes ten rooted oriented walks and two units of trace deficit per walk. The number is therefore $40/(4\cdot5)=2$. This count concerns shortest negative cycles; later deficits also include repeated and composite closed walks.

6. Locate the missing hypothesis in a top-root test

Role: Disconnected counterexample. Review: §15.6.

Problem. Verify why a positive $K_4$ component can hide an unbalanced triangle.

Solution. The positive component has top root three. Any signed triangle has absolute radius at most the unsigned triangle radius two, so it cannot exceed three. The block-diagonal signed top root is three, as is the unsigned whole-graph top root. Nevertheless the negative cycle in the triangle remains. The Perron vector of the whole unsigned graph vanishes on the smaller component, so the proof's strictly positive-coordinate step fails. Testing each connected component independently restores the theorem.

7. Balanced and antibalanced can coincide

Role: Bipartite equivalence proof. Review: §15.7.

Problem. Prove the stated bipartite criterion without using eigenvalues.

Solution. If one signature is both, every cycle product is simultaneously one and $(-1)^{|C|}$, so every cycle is even and the support is bipartite. Conversely, in a bipartite graph every cycle is even. Negating all edge signs multiplies each cycle product by one, so a signature is balanced exactly when its negation is balanced. The balanced and antibalanced switching classes therefore coincide. Forests satisfy this criterion vacuously.

8. Derive the negative-square spectrum

Role: Mode-spectrum calculation. Review: §15.7.

Problem. Find the eigenvalues of a four-cycle with one negative edge and compare with the unsigned cycle.

Solution. Normalize the first three path edges positive and the closing edge negative. The antiperiodic modes have angles $\pi/4,3\pi/4,5\pi/4,7\pi/4$. Twice their cosines gives $\sqrt2,-\sqrt2,-\sqrt2,\sqrt2$, so the polynomial is $(x^2-2)^2$. The unsigned cycle has $2,0,0,-2$. Thus this unbalanced even cycle has absolute radius $\sqrt2\lt 2$ and is not antibalanced, since an even antibalanced cycle would have positive product.

9. A gauge change is not evidence of social evolution

Role: Model critique. Review: §15.3.

Problem. What does switching a balanced signed network to all positive establish about its modeled interactions?

Solution. It establishes the existence of vertex potentials that factor every observed edge sign. In matrix terms it changes coordinate signs by an invertible diagonal matrix and preserves cycle products and spectra. It does not establish that individuals physically changed relationships, that the network will converge to friendship, or that a chosen social model fits observed data. Such claims need a separately specified dynamics and empirical evidence. The mathematical balance certificate remains exact for the stipulated static signature.

15.10 The invariant is a cycle product

Switching removes local sign choices while retaining global consistency constraints. A forest supplies coordinates, its chords supply independent cycle tests, and Perron equality turns a single top eigenvalue into a complete balance certificate only when every vertex lies in the same connected support. The next chapter replaces adjacency by a positive-semidefinite signed Laplacian and asks how much alteration is needed when the cycle constraints conflict.
