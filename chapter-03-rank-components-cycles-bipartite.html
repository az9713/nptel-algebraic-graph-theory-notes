3. Rank, components, cycles and bipartite structure

The six links in our five-device network carry only four independent vertex imbalances. The other two degrees of freedom circulate around loops without injecting or removing anything. Matrix rank turns that distinction into an exact accounting identity.

Throughout the main arguments, $G=(V,E)$ is a finite simple undirected graph with $n\ge1$ vertices, $m$ edges and $c$ connected components. All vector spaces and ranks are over $\mathbb R$. Give each edge an arbitrary orientation and form the tail-positive incidence matrix $B$, as in Chapter 2. Its dimensions are $n\times m$. We will also use the unsigned incidence matrix $U$, with a $1$ at each endpoint of every edge. Substituting $U$ for $B$ changes the theorem; keeping their names separate matters.

Lectures 5 and 6 develop the linear-algebra preliminaries, incidence rank, cycle dependence and the connected-graph unsigned-incidence criterion. Their complete machine transcripts were read in order. The fundamental-cycle construction, flow problem, disconnected counterexamples and explicit proofs below are original exposition. No full audit of the original board is claimed, and uncertain spoken indices are replaced by dimensionally consistent derivations.

3.1 Independence means no nontrivial zero combination 01:01

For vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ in a real vector space, a linear combination is $\sum_i\alpha_i v_i$ with real coefficients. Their span is the set of all such combinations. The list is linearly independent if $\sum_i\alpha_iv_i=0$ forces every coefficient to be zero. Otherwise it is dependent. A zero coefficient is allowed in a dependency; dependence requires at least one nonzero coefficient, not that every coefficient be nonzero.

The vectors $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are independent because the two coordinates recover their coefficients. Adding $(1,1)$ gives a dependent list, since $(1,0)+(0,1)-(1,1)=0$. This does not say that any three planar vectors allow each of them to be expressed using the other two. For the list $(1,0),(2,0),(0,1)$, the third cannot be so expressed. What dependence guarantees is that at least one vector can be expressed using the others: solve the zero combination for a vector with nonzero coefficient.

A basis of a space is an independent spanning list, and its length is the space's dimension. A maximal independent subset of a given finite list spans that list; otherwise one could adjoin an unspanned vector and remain independent. “Maximal” here means that no further listed vector can be adjoined. It is a property to prove, not an instruction to count all nonzero columns. Distinct nonzero columns can still be dependent.

For a matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$, its column space is $\{Mx:x\in\mathbb R^q\}$, and its column rank is the dimension of that space. The row rank is the dimension of the span of its rows. They agree. One way to verify this basic fact is to reduce $M$ to reduced row-echelon form $R$ using invertible elementary row operations. Such operations preserve row rank because every new row is a combination of old rows and the inverse gives the reverse containment of spans. They preserve all column dependencies because $M x=0$ if and only if $R x=0$.

If $R$ has $r$ pivot rows, its $r$ nonzero rows are independent: in the pivot column of a given row only that row has entry one, so a zero row combination has each coefficient zero. Its pivot columns are independent and span every column, since all nonzero entries lie in those $r$ rows and the pivot columns supply their coordinate vectors. Hence both ranks equal $r$. We write this common number as $\operatorname{rank}M$.

3.2 Keep the two kernels in the correct spaces 08:15

The right kernel is $\ker M=\{x\in\mathbb R^q:Mx=0\}$. The left kernel, represented by column vectors, is $\ker M^T=\{y\in\mathbb R^p:M^Ty=0\}$. In the latter equation one can equivalently write $y^TM=0^T$. These kernels have different ambient dimensions; for incidence, they describe edges and vertices respectively.

Rank–nullity. For $M$ of size $p\times q$,

$$\operatorname{rank}M+\dim\ker M=q,\qquad \operatorname{rank}M+\dim\ker M^T=p.$$

To prove the first identity, choose a basis $z_1,\ldots,z_d$ for the kernel and extend it to a basis $z_1,\ldots,z_d,w_1,\ldots,w_{q-d}$ of $\mathbb R^q$. The images $Mw_j$ span the column space because the $z_i$ map to zero. They are independent: if $\sum_j\beta_jMw_j=0$, then $\sum_j\beta_jw_j$ lies in the kernel, hence is a combination of the $z_i$. Independence of the extended basis forces every $\beta_j=0$. Thus the image dimension is $q-d$. Apply the same argument to $M^T$ and use equality of row and column rank for the second identity.

For $B\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$, the left kernel consists of vertex potentials whose edge differences vanish; the right kernel consists of signed edge fluxes with zero imbalance. A dimension missing from one of these spaces does not vanish physically: it has been excluded by the model's constraints. In particular a connected graph's incidence matrix never has row rank $n$, since the all-ones vertex vector is always in its left kernel.

3.3 Every component contributes one constant potential 13:15

Let the connected components have vertex sets $V_1,\ldots,V_c$. Define their indicator vectors $\mathbf1_{V_a}\in\mathbb R^n$ by entry $1$ on $V_a$ and $0$ elsewhere. Because every edge stays within one component, $B^T\mathbf1_{V_a}=0$. These $c$ vectors are independent: at a vertex of $V_a$, a linear combination of them reads off exactly the coefficient of $\mathbf1_{V_a}$.

Conversely, if $B^Tx=0$, then for each oriented edge $u\to v$ we have $x_u-x_v=0$. Following a path shows that $x$ is constant on each connected component. Calling that value $\alpha_a$ gives $x=\sum_a\alpha_a\mathbf1_{V_a}$. Therefore

$$\ker B^T=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf1_{V_1},\ldots,\mathbf1_{V_c}\},\qquad \operatorname{rank}B=n-c.$$

The rank conclusion follows from left rank–nullity. Connectedness is equivalent to $c=1$, hence to $\operatorname{rank}B=n-1$. This includes the single-vertex graph: $n=1,m=0,c=1$ and the $1\times0$ matrix has rank zero. If a graph has no edges at all, every vertex is its own component and the formula again gives rank zero.

In the running graph, $n=5,c=1$, so incidence rank is $4$. Removing the pendant edge $24$ leaves the square-plus-diagonal component and isolated vertex $4$. Then $n=5,c=2$ and the rank falls to $3$. Removing only the diagonal $02$ preserves connectedness and rank $4$, although the graph loses a cycle. Rank counts connectivity constraints; it does not count all available edges.

In a disconnected graph, the single equation $\sum_vx_v=0$ is not equivalent to having fixed the arbitrary constant in every component. Each component supplies its own constant direction. To obtain a unique potential representation one can prescribe one reference potential per component. This is the algebraic version of choosing a separate zero level in each disconnected subsystem.

3.4 The Laplacian has the same real rank 29:53

The identity $L=BB^T$ does not justify multiplying ranks as if they were scalars. The correct argument identifies kernels. If $B^Tx=0$, clearly $Lx=0$. Conversely, if $Lx=0$, multiply by $x^T$ to obtain $0=x^TLx=\|B^Tx\|_2^2$. Over the real numbers, a sum of squares is zero only when every term is zero. Hence $B^Tx=0$. Thus

$$\ker L=\ker B^T,\qquad \dim\ker L=c,\qquad \operatorname{rank}L=n-c.$$

It is important to distinguish $\ker L$ from $\ker B$: the former lives on vertices and has dimension $c$, while the latter lives on edges and will have dimension $m-n+c$. Even when an example accidentally makes those numbers equal, the spaces represent different objects.

The real-field hypothesis was used in the sum-of-squares step. It must not be silently transferred to an arbitrary finite field. Nor does the product formula alone imply $\operatorname{rank}(XY)=\min(\operatorname{rank}X,\operatorname{rank}Y)$ for general matrices. For example, $X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $Y=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ each have rank one, while $XY$ is zero. The special transpose factorization over $\mathbb R$ supplies the needed extra structure.

3.5 Independent incidence columns are exactly forests 00:48

Choose any edge subset $F\subseteq E$. The matrix $B[:,F]$ retains all vertices and the selected columns. Its graph is the spanning edge-selected subgraph $(V,F)$, not necessarily the induced subgraph on the endpoints. A forest is a graph with no cycle; its nontrivial components are trees and its isolated vertices are trees with one vertex.

Column-independence theorem. The columns of $B[:,F]$ are linearly independent if and only if $(V,F)$ is a forest.

A cycle creates a dependency. Traverse a cycle once. Give each selected cycle edge coefficient $+1$ if its chosen orientation agrees with the traversal and $-1$ if it disagrees; give every other edge coefficient zero. At each cycle vertex, one traversal edge enters and one leaves. Their signed incidence contributions cancel. At every other vertex the contribution is zero. This produces a nonzero vector $z$ supported on the cycle with $Bz=0$. Hence the selected columns are dependent.

A forest permits no dependency. Suppose a combination of selected columns is zero. If there is a selected edge, some nontrivial tree component has a leaf. In that leaf's row, only its incident edge column is nonzero, with entry $+1$ or $-1$. The zero-combination equation forces that edge's coefficient to be zero. Delete the leaf edge and repeat on the smaller forest. Eventually every edge coefficient has been forced to zero. If there are no selected edges, the empty list is independent by definition. This proves the converse without needing a nonsquare determinant.

The proof yields a useful distinction: deleting a redundant cycle edge need not change rank, while deleting a bridge does. A bridge is an edge whose deletion increases the component count. Since $n$ remains fixed, $\operatorname{rank}B=n-c$ drops by one when a bridge is removed. If an edge lies on a cycle, its endpoints remain joined by the rest of the cycle after deletion; every old path using it can be rerouted, so no component splits and the rank stays the same. Conversely an edge not on a cycle has no alternate endpoint path and is a bridge.

In particular, the largest independent edge subsets are spanning forests: choose a tree inside each component. They have $\sum_a(|V_a|-1)=n-c$ edges. Adding any other edge to such a forest closes a cycle. Here “spanning forest” means one spanning tree in each original component; an arbitrary forest using all vertices may contain fewer edges and need not be maximal.

3.6 The missing edge dimensions form a cycle space

A vector $f\in\mathbb R^m$ with $Bf=0$ is a circulation. Its entries are signed fluxes relative to the chosen orientation. The set $\ker B$ is the real cycle space. From rank–nullity,

$$\dim\ker B=m-\operatorname{rank}B=m-n+c.$$

This is the cycle rank, not the number of all simple cycles, which may be much larger. We can construct a basis and thereby see the dimension directly.

Fix a spanning forest $T$. Each edge $e\notin T$, called a chord relative to this forest, joins two vertices in one component. In $T$ those vertices have a unique path. Traverse $e$ in its chosen orientation and then return along that tree path. Give the traversal its signed coefficients as above, defining $z^{(e)}$. Its chord coordinate is $z^{(e)}_e=1$, and all other chord coordinates are zero. Consequently the vectors $z^{(e)}$ are independent: the coordinate of a particular chord reads off its coefficient in any zero combination.

For any circulation $f$, subtract $\sum_{e\notin T} f_ez^{(e)}$. The difference is still a circulation and has zero coordinates on every chord. It is therefore supported on a forest. The leaf argument from Section 3.5 forces it to be zero. Thus the vectors span the cycle space. There are $m-(n-c)$ chords, confirming the dimension formula with an explicit basis.

For the running graph use tree edges $T=\{01,12,23,24\}$ and chords $03,02$. With edge order $01,12,23,03,02,24$, a basis is

$$z^{(03)}=(-1,-1,-1,1,0,0)^T,\qquad z^{(02)}=(-1,-1,0,0,1,0)^T.$$

For example $03$ travels $0\to3$, followed by the return path $3\to2\to1\to0$; each return edge opposes its chosen orientation. Both vectors satisfy $Bz=0$. Their chord coordinates isolate the coefficients of a general circulation. The graph has three simple cycles—the square and two triangles—but the real cycle space has dimension only two. A basis describes independent combinations, not a list of all visible loops.

Some texts discuss a binary cycle space over the two-element field, where edge sets combine by symmetric difference. That is a useful related construction, but its coefficients and addition differ from the real signed fluxes here. Stating the field prevents confusing “take an edge twice” with “send twice as much flux.”

3.7 Bipartiteness is a consistency condition on signs 12:45

A graph is bipartite if its vertices can be partitioned into disjoint sets $X,Y$ such that every edge has one endpoint in each. One part may be empty in an edgeless component. Along any path, the parts alternate, so every cycle in a bipartite graph has even length.

Conversely, suppose a connected graph has no odd cycle. Choose a root and a spanning tree, and partition vertices according to even or odd distance from that root in the tree. If every graph edge crosses the partition, it is a bipartition. Otherwise an edge $uv$ joins vertices with the same tree-depth parity. The unique tree path from $u$ to $v$ has even length: its length is $d(u)+d(v)-2d(w)$, where $w$ is the last shared vertex on their paths from the root. The edge $uv$ is not a tree edge, since tree edges change depth parity. Adding it to that even tree path makes an odd simple cycle, a contradiction. Apply this construction separately in each component. Thus bipartiteness is equivalent to the absence of odd cycles.

Now define the unsigned incidence matrix $U\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$ by $U_{ve}=1$ when $v$ is an endpoint of $e$ and $0$ otherwise. Both endpoints receive $+1$, so there is no orientation. The equation $U^Tx=0$ says $x_u+x_v=0$ along each edge, rather than equality of endpoint values. Signs therefore alternate along a path.

In a connected bipartite graph, assigning $+1$ on $X$ and $-1$ on $Y$ gives a nonzero left-kernel vector $s$. Once the value at a root is specified, path propagation fixes every other value, so all left-kernel vectors are multiples of $s$. In a connected graph with an odd cycle, propagating a value $a$ around that cycle returns $a=-a$, forcing $a=0$ over $\mathbb R$. Connectedness propagates this zero to every vertex. These are different kernels, although $B$ and $U$ record the same endpoints.

3.8 Unsigned incidence counts bipartite components 14:58

Connected unsigned-incidence theorem. If $G$ is connected,

$$\operatorname{rank}U=\begin{cases}n-1,&G\text{ is bipartite},\\n,&G\text{ is not bipartite}.\end{cases}$$

The kernel analysis above and left rank–nullity prove this immediately. In the bipartite case it can also be seen by orienting all edges from $X$ to $Y$. Multiplying the rows of $U$ indexed by $Y$ by $-1$ gives that oriented $B$. This is an invertible row operation, so $U$ and $B$ have the same rank $n-1$. A general nonbipartite graph has no globally consistent choice of these vertex signs; an odd cycle obstructs it.

General form. If exactly $b$ of the graph's connected components are bipartite, counting isolated vertices among them, then

$$\dim\ker U^T=b,\qquad\operatorname{rank}U=n-b.$$

Each bipartite component contributes its signed partition vector, extended by zero elsewhere; each nonbipartite component contributes no nonzero left-kernel direction. The component supports are disjoint, so those $b$ vectors form a basis. This proves the general formula. Compare it with $\operatorname{rank}B=n-c$: only $U$ notices odd cycles through the disappearance of a left-kernel direction.

A connected triangle has $\operatorname{rank}B=2$ and $\operatorname{rank}U=3$. A four-cycle has both ranks equal to $3$. Our square-plus-diagonal-plus-leaf graph contains a triangle, so its unsigned incidence has full row rank $5$, while oriented rank is $4$. Full row rank does not mean its six columns are independent; right nullity of $U$ is $6-5=1$.

The connectedness hypothesis cannot be dropped from the first theorem. Take a triangle and a disjoint single edge, giving $n=5,c=2,b=1$. Then $\operatorname{rank}U=4=n-1$, but the graph is disconnected and not bipartite. The formula $n-b$ explains exactly why this is possible. Its oriented incidence rank is $3$, which still correctly reports two components.

The Brouwer–Haemers author-hosted text, §1.1, distinguishes oriented incidence from its $0$–$1$ counterpart and the products $D-A$ and $D+A$. Here $UU^T=D+A$ follows by the same entrywise calculation as in Chapter 2: an adjacent off-diagonal pair now contributes $+1$. All rank proofs and disconnected boundary cases have been supplied above, rather than inferred just from those products.

3.9 An original flow problem: compatibility and minimum norm

Suppose $s_v$ specifies a desired net outward flux at each vertex. With our convention, the balance equation is $Bf=s$. A necessary condition is $\sum_{v\in V_a}s_v=0$ in every connected component, because multiplying by $\mathbf1_{V_a}^T$ annihilates $B$. Global zero sum alone is insufficient if two disconnected components are assigned opposite nonzero total supplies.

This condition is also sufficient. The column space of $B$ is contained in the subspace of vectors with zero component sums. That subspace has dimension $n-c$, since its $c$ constraints are independent. The column space also has dimension $n-c$, by the rank theorem. Containment and equal dimension therefore imply equality. If $f_0$ is one feasible solution, every solution is $f=f_0+z$ with $z\in\ker B$: subtracting two balance equations gives a circulation, and adding a circulation preserves balance.

A particular solution can be selected by minimizing $\|f\|_2^2$. This is a mathematical unit-weight flux cost; no physical resistance data have been supplied. Since the real symmetric Laplacian has the same kernel and rank as $B^T$, its column space is also the component-zero-sum subspace. Hence compatible $s$ permits a solution of $Lx=s$. Fix one vertex value per component to remove the arbitrary constants, and put $f_*=B^Tx$. Then $Bf_*=BB^Tx=Lx=s$. For every circulation $z$,

$$z^Tf_*=z^TB^Tx=(Bz)^Tx=0,\qquad\|f_*+z\|_2^2=\|f_*\|_2^2+\|z\|_2^2.$$

Thus $f_*$ is the unique minimum-norm feasible flux. The potential is unique only after its component constants are fixed; its differences, and therefore $f_*$, are unique regardless. This is an orthogonality statement about gradients and circulations, proved directly from incidence.

For our running graph prescribe $s=(1,0,0,0,-1)^T$ and set $x_2=0$. Solving the four remaining equations gives $x=(1/2,1/4,0,1/4,-1)^T$. The minimum-norm flux in the established edge order is $f_*=(1/4,1/4,-1/4,1/4,1/2,1)^T$, and $\|f_*\|_2^2=3/2$. The negative entry on $23$ means flow opposes the bookkeeping direction, not that the balance law is violated. Adding $z^{(03)}$ preserves $s$ but increases the squared norm by $4$, to $11/2$.

3.10 Exact rank, cycle and flow checks

This SymPy program verifies the running graph and a disconnected triangle–edge–isolated-vertex example. It uses exact rational arithmetic for the potential and flux. It also displays the triangle determinant that distinguishes real unsigned incidence from arithmetic modulo two. The computations have no file or network effects.

from sympy import Matrix, Rational


def incidence(n, edges, unsigned=False):
    matrix = Matrix.zeros(n, len(edges))
    for j, (u, v) in enumerate(edges):
        matrix[u, j] = 1
        matrix[v, j] = 1 if unsigned else -1
    return matrix


edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (0, 3), (0, 2), (2, 4)]
b = incidence(5, edges)
u = incidence(5, edges, unsigned=True)
lap = b*b.T
z03 = Matrix([-1, -1, -1, 1, 0, 0])
z02 = Matrix([-1, -1, 0, 0, 1, 0])
cycles = Matrix.hstack(z03, z02)
assert b*cycles == Matrix.zeros(5, 2) and cycles.rank() == 2
assert b.rank() == lap.rank() == 4 and u.rank() == 5
assert len(b.nullspace()) == 2 and len(b.T.nullspace()) == 1

s = Matrix([1, 0, 0, 0, -1])
x = Matrix([Rational(1, 2), Rational(1, 4), 0, Rational(1, 4), -1])
flow = b.T*x
assert lap*x == s and b*flow == s
assert cycles.T*flow == Matrix.zeros(2, 1)
assert flow.dot(flow) == Rational(3, 2)
assert (flow+z03).dot(flow+z03) == Rational(11, 2)

parts = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (0, 2), (3, 4)]
b_parts = incidence(6, parts)
u_parts = incidence(6, parts, unsigned=True)
assert b_parts.rank() == 3 and u_parts.rank() == 4
triangle_u = incidence(3, parts[:3], unsigned=True)
assert abs(triangle_u.det()) == 2
print('running oriented / Laplacian / unsigned ranks:', b.rank(), lap.rank(), u.rank())
print('left / right oriented nullities:', len(b.T.nullspace()), len(b.nullspace()))
print('fundamental cycle basis size:', cycles.rank())
print('minimum-norm flow:', list(flow))
print('minimum / perturbed squared norms:', flow.dot(flow), '/', (flow+z03).dot(flow+z03))
print('triangle + edge + isolate ranks:', b_parts.rank(), u_parts.rank())
print('absolute unsigned triangle determinant:', abs(triangle_u.det()))
running oriented / Laplacian / unsigned ranks: 4 4 5
left / right oriented nullities: 1 2
fundamental cycle basis size: 2
minimum-norm flow: [1/4, 1/4, -1/4, 1/4, 1/2, 1]
minimum / perturbed squared norms: 3/2 / 11/2
triangle + edge + isolate ranks: 3 4
absolute unsigned triangle determinant: 2

The disconnected example has $n=6,m=4,c=3$ and $b=2$: the single edge and the isolated vertex are bipartite components, while the triangle is not. The ranks $3$ and $4$ are exactly $n-c$ and $n-b$. Its oriented right nullity is one, contributed by the triangle circulation. Zero edge columns are not inserted for isolated vertices; they contribute rows and left-kernel directions instead.

3.11 Problems with complete solutions

  1. Repair a dimension-based claim. Are any three vectors in $\mathbb R^2$ dependent? Must each be a combination of the other two? Give a proof and a counterexample.

    Solution

    A matrix with three columns in $\mathbb R^2$ has rank at most two. Right rank–nullity gives kernel dimension at least one, hence a nontrivial dependence. The claim about each vector is false: take $(1,0),(2,0),(0,1)$. Every combination of the first two has second coordinate zero, so cannot equal the third. The first two do express one another by scaling. A dependence permits solving only for a vector whose coefficient in that dependence is nonzero.

  2. Recover components without drawing. A graph has $n=9,m=8$ and oriented incidence rank $6$. Find the number of components and the dimension of its real cycle space. If one cycle edge is removed, which of these quantities change?

    Solution

    The component count is $c=n-\operatorname{rank}B=9-6=3$. The cycle-space dimension is $m-n+c=8-9+3=2$. A cycle edge has an alternate endpoint path, so removing it preserves components and rank. The edge number falls to $7$, leaving cycle-space dimension $7-9+3=1$. It is incorrect to infer that exactly two simple cycles were present; dimension counts an independent basis, not every simple cycle.

  3. A forest criterion from nullity. Prove that $G$ is a forest if and only if $m=n-c$. State the result for a connected graph and for an edgeless graph.

    Solution

    The selected-column theorem applied to all edges says that a forest is equivalent to independence of all columns, or $\ker B=\{0\}$. Rank–nullity then gives $m=\operatorname{rank}B=n-c$. Conversely, if $m=n-c$, the right nullity is zero, so no cycle dependency exists and the graph is a forest. A connected graph has $c=1$, yielding $m=n-1$ exactly for a tree. An edgeless graph has $m=0,c=n$ and satisfies the equality, including any number of isolated vertices. The component count must be retained; $m=n-1$ alone does not imply connectedness.

  4. One missing hypothesis. Construct a disconnected nonbipartite graph whose unsigned incidence rank is $n-1$. Then add one isolated vertex and compute both oriented and unsigned ranks and both left nullities.

    Solution

    A triangle disjoint from one edge has $n=5,c=2,b=1$. Its oriented rank is $3$ and unsigned rank is $4=n-1$. It is nonbipartite because of the triangle and disconnected by construction. Add an isolated vertex: now $n=6,c=3,b=2$, so oriented rank remains $3$ and unsigned rank remains $4$. The oriented left nullity increases from $2$ to $3$, while the unsigned left nullity increases from $1$ to $2$. The new isolated row contributes a new free vertex value to each kernel but no new independent edge column. This is why the connected unsigned theorem must not be applied merely by inspecting a rank equal to $n-1$.

  5. Resolve a circulation uniquely. In the running graph a circulation has coordinate $f_{03}=2$ and $f_{02}=-1$. Find its other entries and verify that the pendant flux is zero.

    Solution

    The chord coordinates are the coefficients of the fundamental-cycle basis. Thus $f=2z^{(03)}-z^{(02)}=(-1,-1,-2,2,-1,0)^T$ in the order $01,12,23,03,02,24$. Multiplying by $B$ gives zero: at vertex $0$ the outgoing sum is $-1+2-1=0$, at $1$ it is $1-1=0$, at $2$ it is $1-2+1+0=0$, at $3$ it is $2-2=0$, and at $4$ it is $0$. The pendant coordinate is necessarily zero because its leaf has no other incident flux available to balance it. No arbitrary extra forest-supported circulation can be added, by column independence.

  6. Global balance can be insufficient. Let a graph consist of two disjoint edges $01$ and $23$. For $s=(1,0,-1,0)^T$, can $Bf=s$ be solved? What about $s'=(1,-1,-1,1)^T$, orienting each edge from its smaller endpoint to its larger endpoint?

    Solution

    The first vector sums to zero globally, but its component sums are $1$ on $\{0,1\}$ and $-1$ on $\{2,3\}$. No solution exists because no flux can cross the absent connection. For $s'$, each component sum is zero. The two edge fluxes are $f_{01}=1$ and $f_{23}=-1$: the negative second flux gives outward imbalances $-1$ at $2$ and $+1$ at $3$. The solution is unique since two disjoint edges form a forest and $\ker B=0$. Changing either edge's bookkeeping orientation negates its coordinate but leaves the achievable vertex balances unchanged.

  7. The field matters. Write the unsigned incidence matrix of a triangle with edge order $01,12,02$. Compute its determinant over $\mathbb R$. Explain why the same full-rank conclusion fails when arithmetic is modulo two.

    Solution

    The matrix is $U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $2$, so its real rank is $3$. Modulo two, $1=-1$ and the column sums are zero. The first two columns are independent and their sum is the third, so rank is $2$. In the left-kernel argument an odd cycle gives $a=-a$; over $\mathbb R$ this implies $2a=0$ and hence $a=0$, but modulo two that equation holds for every $a$. The theorem in Section 3.8 was stated over the reals precisely to avoid this silent change of arithmetic.

  8. Prove uniqueness of the minimum-cost flux. For compatible $s$, suppose $f_*=B^Tx$ solves $Bf_*=s$. Show that any distinct feasible $g$ has larger squared norm, even when the graph is disconnected. Can the potential $x$ nevertheless be nonunique?

    Solution

    Put $z=g-f_*$. Then $Bz=Bg-Bf_*=s-s=0$. Hence $z^Tf_*=(Bz)^Tx=0$ and $\|g\|_2^2=\|f_*\|_2^2+\|z\|_2^2$. If $g\ne f_*$, then $z\ne0$ and its real squared norm is strictly positive. This proves uniqueness without assuming connectedness. Potentials may differ by any member of $\ker B^T$, that is, independent constants on the components. Such changes leave $B^Tx$ unchanged. Fixing one potential per component makes $x$ unique as well, since a component-constant difference that vanishes at its reference vertex vanishes throughout that component.

3.12 What rank reveals and what remains invisible

Oriented incidence has rank $n-c$ because vertex differences cannot detect component constants. Its right kernel has dimension $m-n+c$ because flux can circulate along independent cycles. Unsigned incidence replaces equality by sign reversal and has rank $n-b$, where $b$ counts bipartite components. These formulas apply to the stated real vector spaces and the complete vertex set, including isolates.

The formulas do not reconstruct a graph from its rank: many different graphs have the same counts. Exact mathematical rank also differs from a floating-point numerical-rank decision near a tolerance. The flow extension uses a chosen unit-weight norm and gives no capacity or reliability guarantee. The next chapters use eigenvalues to extract more structure than rank alone can record, while retaining the same care about which matrix and which hypotheses are in force.
