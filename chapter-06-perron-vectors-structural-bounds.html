6. Perron vectors and structural eigenvalue bounds

The largest eigenvalue of an unsigned graph has a preferred eigenvector: every coordinate can be chosen nonnegative, and connectivity makes every coordinate positive and fixes the direction uniquely. That statement connects a variational maximum to paths, degrees and edge addition. It also has a precise limitation: the negative end of the spectrum can have the same magnitude. We prove the symmetric version directly, then use it to certify bounds and understand when a spectral growth model settles or oscillates.

The arguments, running-network calculations, trial-vector certificates and problems below are original exposition. We restrict the theorem to real symmetric, entrywise nonnegative matrices; no proof of the general nonsymmetric Perron–Frobenius theorem is claimed.

6.1 Absolute values improve a nonnegative quadratic form

Vector norms are Euclidean, and inequalities between vectors mean coordinatewise inequalities. For matrices, the notation $M\ge0$ below also refers to entries; $M\succeq0$ refers instead to nonnegative quadratic forms.

For a real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix $M$, $n\ge1$, list its eigenvalues as $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n$. Write $M\ge0$ to mean every entry is nonnegative. This differs from positive semidefiniteness, which means $x^TMx\ge0$ for every $x$. The adjacency matrix of a single edge is entrywise nonnegative and has eigenvalues1 and−1, so the two conditions cannot be substituted for each other.

Choose a unit eigenvector $v$ for $\lambda_1$. Its coordinatewise absolute value $u=|v|$ has the same norm. Entrywise nonnegativity gives

$$\lambda_1=v^TMv=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}v_iv_j\le\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}|v_i||v_j|=u^TMu\le\lambda_1.$$

The final inequality is the Rayleigh maximum proved in chapter 4. Consequently equality holds throughout. The equality characterization of that maximum says that $u$ belongs to the top eigenspace, so $Mu=\lambda_1u$. This proves existence of a nonnegative nonzero top eigenvector without assuming it in advance. It does not say that every top eigenvector of a disconnected matrix has one sign.

The same idea controls both ends of the spectrum. For any unit eigenvector $z$ with eigenvalue $\mu$,

$$|\mu|=|z^TMz|\le |z|^TM|z|\le\lambda_1.$$

Thus $\lambda_1\ge0$ and equals the spectral radius $\rho(M)=\max_i|\lambda_i|$. This conclusion needs no connectivity. When $M=0$, every eigenvalue is0; that degenerate case is allowed. For an adjacency matrix with at least one edge, the unit vector supported equally on its two endpoints has Rayleigh quotient1, so $\rho\ge1$. The largest eigenvalue is strictly positive in that case.

6.2 Connectivity propagates positivity

Write $G=(V,E)$ and $j\sim i$ when vertices $j,i$ are adjacent. Thus the sum over $j\sim i$ is a sum over neighbors of $i$.

For the rest of the graph statements, $G$ is finite, undirected and simple unless specified. Let $A$ be its adjacency matrix. Suppose $x\ge0$, $x\ne0$, and $Ax=\mu x$. If $x_i=0$, its row equation is

$$0=\mu x_i=\sum_{j\sim i}x_j.$$

Every summand is nonnegative. Hence every neighbor of $i$ also has coordinate0. Continuing along paths forces all coordinates in the connected component of $i$ to vanish. In a connected graph this would force $x=0$, a contradiction. Therefore a nonnegative eigenvector of a connected graph is strictly positive. Notice that this propagation did not require $\mu\gt 0$ or even require that $\mu$ was initially identified as the largest eigenvalue.

Equivalently, if any nonnegative eigenvector has a zero coordinate, the graph must be disconnected. The converse is false as a statement about a particular vector: two disjoint copies of $K_2$ admit the all-ones vector as a strictly positive top eigenvector. Disconnectedness permits different supports; it does not force zeros in every choice.

The same proof works for an entrywise nonnegative symmetric matrix when its support graph is connected: join distinct $i,j$ whenever $M_{ij}\gt 0$. A zero coordinate forces every positive-weight neighbor to vanish because $M_{ij}x_j\ge0$. Diagonal entries do not connect different coordinates. For $n=1$ the support graph is connected by the usual convention, and the sole nonzero eigenvector coordinate is automatically nonzero.

6.3 The positive direction is unique

First consider any top eigenvector $v$ of a connected $G$, with no sign assumption. Section6.1 shows that $|v|$ is also a top eigenvector. Section6.2 makes $|v|$ strictly positive. Therefore every top eigenvector has no zero coordinate. This intermediate statement is what makes the following simplicity proof work.

Suppose there were two independent top eigenvectors $v,w$. Because $w_1\ne0$, the linear combination $y=v-(v_1/w_1)w$ has first coordinate0. It is still in the top eigenspace and it is nonzero by independence. That contradicts the preceding no-zero statement. The top eigenspace is therefore one-dimensional. Symmetry gives diagonalizability, so its algebraic multiplicity is also1; the distinction between geometric and algebraic multiplicity matters for general matrices.

Fix the unique positive unit vector $p$ with $Ap=\rho p$ and $\|p\|_2=1$. Every real top eigenvector is a nonzero real multiple of $p$, hence all of its coordinates have the same strict sign. Finally suppose $Ay=\mu y$ with $y\ge0$, $y\ne0$. Since $p\gt 0$, $p^Ty\gt 0$. Symmetry gives

$$\mu p^Ty=p^TAy=(Ap)^Ty=\rho p^Ty,$$

so $\mu=\rho$. This proves the characterization without vaguely asserting that a nonnegative vector must be orthogonal to something. Both nonzeroness and positivity of the inner product have been established.

For a connected graph these conclusions form the symmetric Perron theorem: the top eigenvalue is simple, its real eigenvectors have one strict sign, every nonnegative nonzero eigenvector belongs to it, and every eigenvalue has magnitude at most it. $K_1$ has $\rho=0$ and positive unit vector(1), so it fits. A connected bipartite graph with an edge also has eigenvalue $-\rho$; simplicity of $+\rho$ is not strict dominance in absolute value.

6.4 Every proper subgraph of a connected graph lowers the top value

The disjoint union $G\sqcup H$ contains the two graphs with no edges between their vertex sets. A nonempty subgraph here has at least one vertex.

Let $H$ be a nonempty subgraph of $G$. It may delete vertices, edges, or both, and it need not be induced. Take a nonnegative unit top eigenvector $x$ of $A_H$ and extend it by zeros to a vector $y$ indexed by all vertices of $G$. Its norm remains1. Every edge retained in $H$ contributes the same nonnegative term to both quadratic forms, while any additional edge of $G$ contributes a nonnegative term. Thus

$$\rho(H)=x^TA_Hx\le y^TA_Gy\le\rho(G).$$

The direction of the entrywise comparison is essential: the zero-padded adjacency matrix of $H$ is at most $A_G$, never the reverse. The intermediate vector need not be strictly positive at this stage.

If $G$ is connected and equality holds at the two ends, then $y$ attains the Rayleigh maximum of $A_G$. It is a top eigenvector, hence strictly positive. Therefore no vertex was deleted. With the same vertex set, the difference is

$$y^T(A_G-A_H)y=2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(G)\setminus E(H)}y_iy_j.$$

Every summand is strictly positive, so equality forces the sum to be empty: no edge was deleted either. Conversely identical graphs have identical spectra. Hence for a connected $G$, $\rho(H)=\rho(G)$ if and only if $H=G$ as a subgraph. This includes deleting an edge while keeping all vertices, even if the deletion disconnects the result.

Connectedness cannot be discarded. In $G=K_3\sqcup K_2$, the proper subgraph $H=K_3$ already has $\rho(H)=\rho(G)=2$. More generally the adjacency matrix is block diagonal over components, so its spectral radius is the largest component radius. Nonnegative top eigenvectors are supported only on components attaining that largest value. Indeed a component with smaller radius cannot contain a nonzero restriction satisfying its top-radius eigenvalue equation. Within each maximizing connected component that restriction is a nonnegative multiple of its positive Perron vector.

6.5 Degrees give bounds, with different equality mechanisms

Here $n=|V|$, $d_i$ is the degree of vertex $i$ and $\mathbf1$ is the length-$n$ all-ones vector.

Let $n\ge1$, $m=|E|$, average degree $\bar d=2m/n$, minimum degree $\delta$ and maximum degree $\Delta$. The all-ones test vector gives

$$\rho\ge\frac{\mathbf1^TA\mathbf1}{\mathbf1^T\mathbf1}=\frac{2m}{n}=\bar d.$$

Equality means that $\mathbf1$ attains the Rayleigh maximum, hence $A\mathbf1=\rho\mathbf1$. The left side is the degree vector, so every vertex has degree $\rho$: the graph is regular. Conversely an $r$-regular graph has $A\mathbf1=r\mathbf1$, and the upper bound below forces $\rho=r$. This lower-bound equality characterizes regularity even for disconnected graphs.

For the upper bound, take a nonnegative top eigenvector and a coordinate $x_i=\max_jx_j\gt 0$. Then

$$\rho x_i=\sum_{j\sim i}x_j\le d_i x_i\le\Delta x_i,$$

so $\rho\le\Delta$. If $G$ is connected and $\rho=\Delta$, all inequalities are equalities. The chosen vertex has degree $\Delta$ and every neighbor also has coordinate $x_i$. At each such neighbor the same row equation forces its degree to be $\Delta$ and its neighbors to attain the same maximum. Connectivity propagates the statement through the graph: it is regular. For disconnected graphs upper-bound equality is weaker: $K_3\sqcup K_1$ has $\rho=\Delta=2$ but is not regular.

A vertex of degree $\Delta\ge1$ and its incident edges give a star subgraph $K_{1,\Delta}$, regardless of additional edges among its neighbors. Chapter 5 derived its top eigenvalue $\sqrt\Delta$. Subgraph monotonicity therefore gives another lower bound. Collecting the inequalities,

$$\boxed{\max\{\bar d,\sqrt\Delta\}\le\rho\le\Delta}. $$

For an edgeless graph set $\Delta=0$; all values are0. For a connected graph with an edge, equality $\rho=\sqrt\Delta$ forces the selected star subgraph to equal the entire graph by strict monotonicity. Hence precisely the stars achieve this lower-bound equality in the connected case. This conclusion would fail without connectedness because an additional component with smaller radius changes neither side.

Our running network has degrees $(3,2,4,2,1)$, $n=5,m=6$. Thus $\bar d=2.4$, $\Delta=4$, and $2.4\le\rho\le4$. Its computed top value is approximately2.685544. The star bound2 is valid but weaker here; bounds should be compared rather than advertised as universally sharper.

6.6 A sharp bound using only vertices and edges

The identity $I=I_n$ acts on vertex coordinates. The trace $\operatorname{tr}A$ is the sum of the diagonal entries of $A$, equivalently the sum of its eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity.

For $n\ge2$, zero diagonal gives $\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i=0$, while $\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=2m$ gives $\sum_i\lambda_i^2=2m$. Cauchy–Schwarz applied directly to $(\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ and the all-ones vector yields

$$\rho^2=\left(\sum_{i=2}^n\lambda_i\right)^2\le(n-1)\sum_{i=2}^n\lambda_i^2=(n-1)(2m-\rho^2).$$

Rearrange to $n\rho^2\le2m(n-1)$ and take the nonnegative square root:

$$\boxed{\rho\le\sqrt{\frac{2m(n-1)}n}}.$$

The real signs can remain in Cauchy–Schwarz; replacing them by absolute values first is possible but creates another equality condition to track. The graph need not be connected. For $n=1$ the sole graph has $m=\rho=0$; verify that case separately rather than dividing by $n-1$.

We can solve the equality case completely. If $m=0$, the graph is empty and equality holds. Suppose $m\gt 0$ and equality holds. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz forces all remaining eigenvalues to have the same value $c=-\rho/(n-1)\lt 0$. Thus the top eigenvalue is simple. For a unit top eigenvector $p$, the spectral decomposition becomes

$$A=cI+(\rho-c)pp^T.$$

Its diagonal entries are0, so $p_i^2=-c/(\rho-c)=1/n$ for every $i$. Choose $p\ge0$ using section6.1, giving $p_i=1/\sqrt n$. Every off-diagonal entry is then the same positive number $(\rho-c)/n$. In a simple graph each such entry is0 or1, so all are1. The graph is $K_n$. Conversely $K_n$ has eigenvalues $n-1,-1,\ldots,-1$ and attains equality. The only equality graphs are the empty and complete graphs, including their overlapping one-vertex case.

6.7 Positive trial vectors give computable certificates

For a vertex $u$, $e_u$ is its coordinate unit vector. In the edge perturbation below, $E=e_ue_v^T+e_ve_u^T$ changes precisely the two symmetric entries associated with the missing edge.

Degree bounds use the particular positive vector $\mathbf1$. An original extension is to replace it with a better-shaped vector $y\gt 0$. Define the row ratios

$$r_i(y)=\frac{(Ay)_i}{y_i},\qquad a=\min_i r_i(y),\quad b=\max_i r_i(y).$$

Then $ay\le Ay\le by$ coordinatewise. Take any nonnegative nonzero top eigenvector $p$, which exists even if $G$ is disconnected. Since $y\gt 0$, $p^Ty\gt 0$. Multiplication by $p^T$ and symmetry give

$$a\,p^Ty\le p^TAy=(Ap)^Ty=\rho p^Ty\le b\,p^Ty.$$

Division proves $a\le\rho\le b$. This is a rigorous certificate when the ratios are evaluated exactly or enclosed by justified intervals. Rounded floating-point ratios alone do not establish an exact certified enclosure.

For the running network choose the integer vector $y=(4,3,5,3,2)^T$. Its row ratios are $(11/4,3,12/5,3,5/2)$, so the certified interval is $[12/5,3]$. This narrows the upper bound4 to3 while retaining the average-degree lower bound2.4. Taking $y=p$ would collapse the interval to the unknown answer; the practical value is that an approximate Perron shape can produce useful bounds before the eigenvalue is known exactly.

There is also a quantitative edge comparison. Add a missing edge $\{u,v\}$ of nonnegative weight $t$ to a connected graph, leaving old weights fixed. With $p$ the old positive unit Perron vector, the Rayleigh test gives

$$\rho(A+t(e_ue_v^T+e_ve_u^T))\ge\rho(A)+2t p_up_v.$$

For $t\gt 0$ this is strict and supplies a lower bound on the increase. It is not an exact finite-change formula because the optimizing vector generally changes. Where a differentiable normalized top eigenpair is followed near $t=0$, differentiation of $Ap=\rho p$ and $p^Tp=1$ gives $\rho'(0)=p^TEp=2p_up_v$; cancellation follows from $p^TA=\rho p^T$. The finite Rayleigh bound itself does not require that differentiability argument. Adding edge $\{1,3\}$ to our network predicts an increase at least approximately0.339352 when $t=1$, with the displayed decimal understood as numerical evaluation of the exact Perron expression.

6.8 Positivity does not remove bipartite oscillation

For a real matrix $B$, $\|B\|_2=\max_{\|x\|_2=1}\|Bx\|_2$ is the induced Euclidean operator norm. For a real symmetric matrix it equals the largest absolute eigenvalue.

Let $G$ be connected with at least two vertices. If it is bipartite, assign signs $+1$ and $-1$ to its parts and let $S$ be the resulting diagonal sign matrix. Every edge joins opposite signs, so $SAS=-A$. From $Ap=\rho p$ we obtain $A(Sp)=-\rho Sp$. Therefore normalized powers $(A/\rho)^k$ preserve a negative mode with alternating sign. Simplicity of $+\rho$ alone does not make the ordinary power iteration converge.

Conversely suppose $Az=-\rho z$. In the inequalities

$$\rho=-z^TAz\le |z|^TA|z|\le\rho\|z\|^2=\rho$$

we have normalized $\|z\|=1$. Equality makes $|z|$ a positive top eigenvector, so no coordinate of $z$ vanishes. Equality in the first inequality means that every edge product $z_iz_j$ is negative: each contributes $-2z_iz_j\le2|z_i||z_j|$, and all nonnegative differences must vanish. The positive and negative coordinates therefore form a bipartition. We have proved that a connected graph with an edge has eigenvalue $-\rho$ exactly when it is bipartite.

For a connected nonbipartite graph all other eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda_i|\lt \rho$: simplicity excludes another $+\rho$, and the preceding argument excludes $-\rho$. The orthonormal spectral expansion then proves

$$\left(\frac A\rho\right)^k\longrightarrow pp^T,\qquad \left\|\left(\frac A\rho\right)^k-pp^T\right\|_2=\max_{i\ge2}\left|\frac{\lambda_i}{\rho}\right|^k.$$

The norm identity follows by diagonalizing the symmetric difference; its eigenvalues are0 in the $p$ direction and $(\lambda_i/\rho)^k$ on the orthogonal directions. A repeated multiplication of a signal $x$ approaches $p(p^Tx)$, with the coefficient depending on the input. This is a statement about adjacency growth, not yet about conservation of probability.

For either bipartite or nonbipartite connected graphs with an edge, $T=(I+A/\rho)/2$ has eigenvalues $(1+\lambda_i/\rho)/2$ in $[0,1]$, with1 simple and all the others strictly below1. Thus $T^k\to pp^T$; the troublesome−1 mode becomes0. This averaging fixes spectral parity but $T$ is generally not a stochastic matrix, because its row sums are not generally1. The random-walk chapter will introduce the different normalization needed for probability transport.

6.9 Execute bounds, strict comparison and parity checks

The following original listing uses NumPy for a numerical eigensolve and SymPy for exact rational row-ratio checks. Assertions validate finite examples and identities; the proofs above provide the universal claims. Decimals are approximations. The disconnected counterexamples protect the equality hypotheses, and the star calculation exposes the negative peripheral mode.

import numpy as np
from sympy import Matrix, Rational

edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (0, 3), (0, 2), (2, 4)]
A = np.zeros((5, 5))
for i, j in edges:
    A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(A)
rho, p = values[-1], vectors[:, -1]
p *= np.sign(p.sum())
assert np.all(p > 0) and np.linalg.norm(A @ p - rho * p) < 1e-12
exact = Matrix(A.astype(int))
y = Matrix([4, 3, 5, 3, 2])
ratios = [v / y[i] for i, v in enumerate(exact * y)]
assert ratios == [Rational(11, 4), 3, Rational(12, 5), 3, Rational(5, 2)]
assert 2.4 <= rho <= 3
print(f"Running graph radius: {rho:.6f}")
print("Exact trial ratios:", ratios)
print(f"Positive unit Perron vector: {np.round(p, 6).tolist()}")
B = A.copy()
B[1, 3] = B[3, 1] = 1
increase = np.linalg.eigvalsh(B)[-1] - rho
bound = 2 * p[1] * p[3]
assert increase >= bound - 1e-12
print(
    f"Added edge 1-3: increase {increase:.6f}; Rayleigh lower "
    f"bound {bound:.6f}"
)
# Every single-edge deletion is a proper spanning subgraph.
for i, j in edges:
    H = A.copy()
    H[i, j] = H[j, i] = 0
    assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)[-1] < rho - 1e-9
print("All six single-edge deletions lower the radius")
# K3 plus an isolate: upper degree equality without global regularity.
D = np.zeros((4, 4))
D[:3, :3] = np.ones((3, 3)) - np.eye(3)
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.eigvalsh(D)[-1], 2)
assert list(D.sum(axis=1)) == [2, 2, 2, 0]
print(
    "Disconnected upper-bound counterexample: radius=Delta=2, "
    "degrees=[2,2,2,0]"
)
# A three-leaf star has radius sqrt(3) and an equally large negative mode.
S = np.zeros((4, 4))
S[0, 1:] = 1
S[1:, 0] = 1
sw, sv = np.linalg.eigh(S)
star_rho = sw[-1]
assert np.isclose(sw[0], -star_rho)
q = np.array([1.0, -1 / np.sqrt(3), -1 / np.sqrt(3), -1 / np.sqrt(3)])
assert np.allclose(S @ q, -star_rho * q)
assert np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(S / star_rho, 4) @ q, q)
assert np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(S / star_rho, 5) @ q, -q)
T = (np.eye(4) + S / star_rho) / 2
assert np.allclose(T @ q, 0)
print(
    "Star: raw powers alternate on the negative mode; averaged "
    "operator kills it"
)
for n in range(2, 8):
    K = np.ones((n, n)) - np.eye(n)
    m = n * (n - 1) // 2
    assert np.isclose(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K)[-1], np.sqrt(2 * m * (n - 1) / n))
print("Complete graphs K2 through K7 attain the edge-count bound")

6.10 Problems with complete solutions

  1. A zero is a statement about a component. For $G=K_3\sqcup K_2$, describe every nonnegative top eigenvector. Can the graph have a strictly positive eigenvector for its largest eigenvalue? Compare with $K_2\sqcup K_2$.

    Solution

    Role: Disconnected support. Review: §6.4.

    The component spectra are $(2,-1,-1)$ and $(1,-1)$, so the whole graph has top value2. On the $K_2$ component the equation $A_2x=2x$ has only the zero solution because2 is not an eigenvalue. On $K_3$ the top eigenspace consists of constant vectors. Hence all nonnegative top eigenvectors are $(a,a,a,0,0)$ with $a\gt 0$, and none is strictly positive. For two copies of $K_2$, both components have top value1. The nonnegative top eigenvectors are $(a,a,b,b)$ with $a,b\ge0$ and $a+b\gt 0$. Taking $a,b\gt 0$ gives a strictly positive vector, but the eigenspace is two-dimensional. Positivity of one vector therefore cannot replace the connectivity hypothesis in the simplicity theorem.

  2. One-star certificate. Find the positive unit Perron vector of $K_{1,q}$ for integer $q\ge1$, and verify $\rho=\sqrt q$. For which $q$ does it attain the average-degree lower bound and the maximum-degree upper bound?

    Solution

    Role: Exact star certificate. Review: §6.5.

    Let $a$ be the central coordinate and $b$ each leaf coordinate. The eigenvalue equations are $\rho a=qb$ and $\rho b=a$, so $\rho=\sqrt q$ and $a=\sqrt q\,b$. Normalization $a^2+qb^2=1$ gives $a=1/\sqrt2$ and $b=1/\sqrt{2q}$, all positive. The average degree is $2q/(q+1)$ and $\Delta=q$. Equality of $\sqrt q$ with $q$ forces $q=1$. Equality with $2q/(q+1)$ gives $q+1=2\sqrt q$, equivalently $(\sqrt q-1)^2=0$, again $q=1$. Larger stars attain the different lower bound $\sqrt\Delta$ while attaining neither degree extreme.

  3. A subgraph need not be induced. Compare $C_3$ with the path obtained by removing one of its edges while retaining all three vertices. Explain exactly where positivity enters the strictness proof.

    Solution

    Role: Strictness proof. Review: §6.4.

    The triangle has radius2. The resulting $P_3$ has radius $\sqrt2$, as derived from the path formula in chapter 5. It is a spanning subgraph and is not induced on its retained vertex set. For the general proof, extend a nonnegative unit Perron vector of the subgraph by zeros. If the radii were equal, the extension would maximize the parent's Rayleigh quotient, so connectedness of the parent would make every coordinate positive. Deleted vertices are then impossible, and any deleted edge contributes a strictly positive term $2y_iy_j$ to the difference of quadratic forms. Without nonnegativity, additional edges could contribute negative terms; without connectedness, a deleted component might have zero Perron mass.

  4. Which degree equality survives disconnectedness? Prove that $\rho=\bar d$ forces regularity for every simple graph. Give a disconnected graph with $\rho=\Delta$ that is not regular, and explain the different equality logic.

    Solution

    Role: Equality hypotheses. Review: §6.5.

    For the first claim, equality of the all-ones Rayleigh quotient with the maximum makes $\mathbf1$ a top eigenvector. Its row equations say $d_i=\rho$ for every vertex, with no connectivity requirement. Conversely regularity makes the lower and upper bounds equal. For the second claim take $K_3\sqcup K_1$, whose degrees are $(2,2,2,0)$ and whose radius is2. The maximum-coordinate proof of the upper bound only constrains the component on which the chosen Perron vector has positive maximum. Propagation cannot reach the isolate, so equality does not require its degree to be2.

  5. Repair an overstrong growth claim. A claim says that every connected graph satisfies $(A/\rho)^k\to pp^T$. Give a counterexample with at least three vertices, then state and prove the repaired claim and an averaging alternative.

    Solution

    Role: Convergence counterexample. Review: §6.8.

    Take $P_3=K_{1,2}$ with $\rho=\sqrt2$. The nonzero vector $q=(1,-\sqrt2,1)^T$ satisfies $Aq=-\sqrt2q$, so $(A/\rho)^kq=(-1)^kq$ never converges. For a connected nonbipartite graph, symmetry gives an orthonormal eigenbasis, simplicity makes the top mode unique, and section6.8 excludes $-\rho$. Thus every other eigenvalue ratio has magnitude strictly below1, and the spectral expansion converges to $pp^T$. For any connected graph with an edge, the averaged operator $T=(I+A/\rho)/2$ has simple eigenvalue1 and all remaining eigenvalues in $[0,1)$, giving $T^k\to pp^T$ even in the bipartite case. Neither normalization is generally a probability-transition matrix.

  6. Turn an approximate shape into an exact enclosure. For the running graph, use $y=(4,3,5,3,2)^T$ to enclose $\rho$. Why is strict positivity of the trial vector required? What happens with $y=\mathbf1$?

    Solution

    Role: Exact enclosure. Review: §6.7.

    The neighbor sums are $(11,9,12,9,5)^T$, giving ratios $(11/4,3,12/5,3,5/2)$. Their minimum and maximum are $12/5$ and3. The nonnegative top vector $p$ has $p^Ty\gt 0$, and the ratio argument therefore certifies $12/5\le\rho\le3$. Strictly positive coordinates make every ratio well-defined and guarantee the positive inner product even for a disconnected graph whose Perron vector may vanish elsewhere. For $\mathbf1$ the ratios are the degrees, giving $\delta\le\rho\le\Delta$, or $1\le\rho\le4$ here. The separate average-degree Rayleigh bound improves the lower side to2.4 without needing a better vector.

  7. Track the sharp edge-count equality. A simple graph on $n\ge2$ vertices with $m\gt 0$ attains $\rho^2=2m(n-1)/n$. Prove it must be complete without assuming connectivity.

    Solution

    Role: Extremal classification. Review: §6.6.

    Cauchy–Schwarz equality gives $\lambda_2=\cdots=\lambda_n=c=-\rho/(n-1)\lt 0$. The top value differs from $c$ and is simple, so $A=cI+(\rho-c)pp^T$ for a nonnegative unit top vector $p$. Zero diagonal yields $p_i^2=-c/(\rho-c)=1/n$ at every vertex, making $p$ strictly positive even though connectivity was not assumed. Then every off-diagonal entry equals $(\rho-c)/n\gt 0$. A simple adjacency matrix only permits entries0 or1, so every off-diagonal entry is1, proving $G=K_n$. If $m=0$, the argument with $c\lt 0$ fails as it should: the empty graph is the other equality case.

  8. Separate edge benefit from an exact formula. In $K_3$, normalize $p=\mathbf1/\sqrt3$. Add a fourth vertex with no edges, then connect it to one triangle vertex. Why does using the old padded $p$ give only a zero lower bound, although the radius increases? Which hypothesis is missing from the strict quantitative argument?

    Solution

    Role: Perturbation boundary. Review: §6.7.

    The disconnected intermediate graph $K_3\sqcup K_1$ has radius2 and padded Perron vector $(1,1,1,0)^T/\sqrt3$. For the newly added edge the product $p_up_v$ is0, so the old-vector Rayleigh estimate only says that the radius does not decrease. The resulting triangle-with-leaf graph is connected, and the intermediate graph is its proper spanning subgraph. Applying strict subgraph monotonicity with that connected graph as parent proves its radius is strictly greater than2. The quantitative estimate's strict positive product assumed the old graph was connected; it was not. A variational lower bound can be non-sharp, and a zero bound does not prove a zero actual increase.

  9. Positive support and numerical centrality. For the running graph's top unit vector approximately $(0.523683,0.411917,0.582539,0.411917,0.216917)$, compare the predicted finite benefit of adding edge $\{1,3\}$ with the derivative interpretation. Explain why the vector is not a probability stationary distribution merely because its coordinates are positive.

    Solution

    Role: Model interpretation. Review: §6.8.

    The unit normalization gives the finite Rayleigh bound $\rho(A+E)-\rho(A)\ge2p_1p_3$, approximately $2(0.411917)^2=0.339352$. The same expression is the first derivative for an infinitesimal edge-weight change when following the differentiable simple top eigenpair; it need not equal the finite increase at weight1. The finite inequality uses the exact $p$, with the decimal only an approximate evaluation, not a rigorous rounded lower endpoint. Positivity and unit Euclidean norm do not imply that coordinates sum to1 or that an adjacency update conserves their sum. A stationary probability vector must belong to an explicitly defined stochastic evolution. Even after rescaling $p$ to have sum1, the equation $Ap=\rho p$ describes adjacency growth, not the stationary equation for the ordinary degree-normalized random walk.

6.11 What the positive mode tells us

Entrywise nonnegativity permits absolute-value comparison; connectivity propagates strict positivity; symmetry supplies orthogonal eigenvectors, the Rayleigh principle and algebraic simplicity. Each hypothesis has a separate job. Proper subgraph monotonicity, regularity equality tests and trial-vector certificates are consequences of that chain, rather than unrelated spectral tricks.

The preferred positive direction controls adjacency growth, but it does not encode every signal and does not eliminate bipartite parity. Exact certificates, approximate eigensolves and experimental centrality interpretations also have different evidential roles. The next chapter develops interlacing and quotient matrices, giving information about several eigenvalues and compressed substructures rather than only the top mode.
