7. Interlacing, positive semidefiniteness and quotient matrices

Deleting a vertex does not simply remove one eigenvalue from a graph's spectrum. It leaves a smaller symmetric matrix whose ordered eigenvalues are trapped between old ones. The reason is geometric: a sufficiently large spectral subspace must meet the retained coordinate space. We build that intersection proof carefully, then use it to analyze bipartite structure, positive quadratic forms and block partitions. A final original extension replaces individual coordinates by normalized group indicators and shows exactly when a compressed network retains genuine eigenmodes.

Evidence and conventions. Lessons 16–19 provide the topic spine; all four complete local machine transcripts were read. ASR is fallible and no complete board or audiovisual transcription is claimed. The proofs, numerical examples, quotient extension and worked solutions are original exposition. Every matrix in a spectral inequality is real symmetric unless a different form is explicitly identified. Eigenvalues are ordered decreasingly and counted with multiplicity.

7.1 Principal submatrices retain the same coordinate indices

A principal submatrix $B=M[U,U]$ keeps the rows and columns of a common index set $U$, with $|U|=m$. Keeping rows $U$ and different columns $V$ is not the same operation, even if the result happens to be square. A principal submatrix of a symmetric matrix is symmetric. For an adjacency matrix it represents the induced graph on $U$, because every edge between retained vertices remains present.

Relabeling coordinates lets us put $B$ in the upper-left corner without changing eigenvalues. Let $P$ be a permutation matrix: its columns are permuted coordinate vectors, so $P^TP=I$. The relabeled matrix is $P^TMP$. If $Mv=\lambda v$, then $P^TMP(P^Tv)=\lambda P^Tv$, and $P^Tv\ne0$ because $P$ is invertible. Applying the inverse permutation proves the reverse correspondence, including eigenspace dimensions. The characteristic polynomial is also unchanged because

$$\det(zI-P^TMP)=\det(P^T(zI-M)P)=\det(P)^2\det(zI-M)=\det(zI-M).$$

Thus we may write $M=\begin{pmatrix}B&C\\C^T&D\end{pmatrix}$. Extending $y\in\mathbb R^m$ by zeros gives $\widetilde y=(y,0)^T$ and

$$\widetilde y^TM\widetilde y=y^TBy,\qquad \|\widetilde y\|=\|y\|.$$

The omitted blocks do not disappear from the full eigenvector equation: its lower part is $C^Ty$, which need not vanish. Equality of the two Rayleigh quotients does not claim that every smaller-matrix eigenvector lifts to a full eigenvector.

7.2 A dimension count forces a nonzero common vector

For subspaces $V,W\subseteq\mathbb R^n$,

$$\dim(V\cap W)=\dim V+\dim W-\dim(V+W)\ge\dim V+\dim W-n.$$

To justify the identity, take a basis of $V\cap W$, extend it to a basis of $V$ and to a basis of $W$, then combine the two extensions with the common basis. The combined list spans $V+W$ and is independent: a dependence would place a nontrivial combination of one extension in the intersection, contradicting how that extension was chosen. Counting its vectors gives the formula. In particular dimensions summing to $n+1$ force an intersection of dimension at least1. Merely saying that the spaces share the zero vector proves nothing; every pair does.

Let $M$ have orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n$. On $\operatorname{span}(u_k,\ldots,u_n)$ every nonzero Rayleigh quotient is at most $\lambda_k$, because its spectral expansion is a weighted average of those lower values. On $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ every such quotient is at least $\lambda_k$. Equal or repeated eigenvalues cause no difficulty.

Let $B$ have eigenvalues $\beta_1\ge\cdots\ge\beta_m$ and an orthonormal eigenbasis $v_1,\ldots,v_m$. Embed the span of $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ by zero padding. It has dimension $k$, while the lower spectral space of $M$ has dimension $n-k+1$. Their dimensions sum to $n+1$, so choose a common nonzero vector $\widetilde y$. Its Rayleigh quotient is at least $\beta_k$ by the $B$ expansion and at most $\lambda_k$ by the $M$ expansion. Hence $\beta_k\le\lambda_k$.

7.3 Both sides of Cauchy interlacing

For the other side, embed the span of $v_k,\ldots,v_m$, of dimension $m-k+1$. Intersect it with $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\ldots,u_{k+n-m})$, of dimension $k+n-m$. Again the sum is $n+1$, so a nonzero intersection vector exists. Its quotient is at most $\beta_k$ from the smaller matrix and at least $\lambda_{k+n-m}$ from the larger one. We obtain

$$\boxed{\lambda_k\ge\beta_k\ge\lambda_{k+n-m},\qquad 1\le k\le m.}$$

The shift is the number $n-m$ of removed coordinates. Removing one coordinate gives $\lambda_k\ge\beta_k\ge\lambda_{k+1}$, the alternating pattern behind the name. Removing none gives equality. A one-dimensional retained matrix has its sole diagonal value between the old minimum and maximum. We assume $1\le m\le n$ so the eigenvalue indices exist.

For the running network, deleting leaf4 leaves the four-vertex graph consisting of a square with diagonal0–2. Its eigenvalues are $\tfrac{1+\sqrt{17}}2,0,-1,\tfrac{1-\sqrt{17}}2$, approximately $(2.561553,0,-1,-1.561553)$. The original values are approximately $(2.685544,0.334904,0,-1.271330,-1.749118)$. Each new value lies in its one-deletion interval. These approximate lists illustrate the exact theorem; the numerical rounding is not its proof.

Arbitrary edge deletion does not satisfy this indexed statement. $C_4$ has spectrum $(2,0,0,-2)$. Remove one edge but retain all vertices: the resulting $P_4$ has spectrum $(\varphi,\varphi^{-1},-\varphi^{-1},-\varphi)$, where $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$. Its second eigenvalue is positive while the old second eigenvalue is0. There is no contradiction: it is not a principal submatrix. Chapter 6's top-radius monotonicity still applies to this subgraph, but cannot be substituted for full interlacing.

7.4 The same geometry gives a variational formula

A useful original reformulation is

$$\lambda_k=\max_{\dim V=k}\ \min_{x\in V\setminus\{0\}}R_M(x) =\min_{\dim W=n-k+1}\ \max_{x\in W\setminus\{0\}}R_M(x),\quad R_M(x)=\frac{x^TMx}{x^Tx}.$$

For the first formula, every $k$-dimensional $V$ intersects $\operatorname{span}(u_k,\ldots,u_n)$ nontrivially, so its minimum quotient is at most $\lambda_k$. The choice $V=\operatorname{span}(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ attains $\lambda_k$. For the second formula, every $(n-k+1)$-dimensional $W$ intersects $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ nontrivially, so its maximum is at least $\lambda_k$; the lower spectral span attains it. Minima and maxima within each fixed subspace exist on its compact unit sphere. These observations prove the formulas rather than merely naming a min–max principle.

More generally let $S$ be any $n\times m$ real matrix with $S^TS=I_m$. Its columns form an orthonormal family, and $B=S^TMS$ is the compression to their span. For $y\ne0$, $R_B(y)=R_M(Sy)$ and the map preserves dimension and norm. The two intersection proofs above work with this embedded subspace exactly as with coordinate padding. Therefore the same interlacing inequalities hold for every such orthonormal compression. A nonorthogonal column matrix requires a different denominator; skipping that normalization changes the eigenvalue problem.

7.5 A bipartite spectrum is symmetric as a multiset

For a bipartite graph with parts of sizes $p,q$, simultaneous permutation yields

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\R^T&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S=\operatorname{diag}(I_p,-I_q),\quad SAS=-A.$$

If $Ax=\lambda x$, then $A(Sx)=-\lambda(Sx)$. The invertible sign map gives a bijection between the $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ eigenspaces. Symmetry of $A$ makes algebraic and geometric multiplicities equal. Thus eigenvalues occur in opposite pairs with equal multiplicity; zeros can remain unpaired. Merely finding that the set of distinct values contains negatives is a weaker condition than the multiset statement used below.

Conversely suppose the adjacency eigenvalue multiset is invariant under $\lambda\mapsto-\lambda$. For every odd integer $r\ge1$,

$$\operatorname{tr}(A^r)=\sum_i\lambda_i^r=0.$$

But $\operatorname{tr}(A^r)$ counts all closed walks of length $r$ and is a sum of nonnegative integer diagonal entries. An odd cycle of length $r$ would contribute at least $2r$ such walks, one per starting point and orientation. Hence no odd cycle exists. The odd-cycle characterization proved in chapter 3 makes the graph bipartite. This works for disconnected graphs and isolates too.

The weaker extreme condition $\lambda_1=-\lambda_n$ characterizes bipartiteness only when the graph is connected, as proved in chapter 6. The disconnected graph $K_{1,9}\sqcup K_3$ has extremes $3$ and−3 supplied by the star, while its triangle component is nonbipartite. Its full multiset is not symmetric: the triangle contributes2 and two−1 values without matching opposites. An extreme equality sees only the component of largest radius.

7.6 Positive semidefiniteness is a quadratic condition

A real symmetric matrix $M$ is positive semidefinite, written $M\succeq0$, if $x^TMx\ge0$ for every $x$. It is positive definite if this is strictly positive for every nonzero$x$. The zero vector must be excluded in the strict definition. Unlike entrywise nonnegativity, these properties concern all directions in a quadratic form.

If $Mu=\lambda u$ with $u\ne0$, then $u^TMu=\lambda\|u\|^2$, so positive semidefiniteness forces $\lambda\ge0$. Conversely the spectral expansion $x=\sum_i c_i u_i$ gives $x^TMx=\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2\ge0$ if all eigenvalues are nonnegative. Strict positivity for every nonzero $x$ is equivalent to all eigenvalues being positive.

The same expansion gives a Gram factorization. If $M=U\Lambda U^T$ with $\Lambda\ge0$, let $C=U\Lambda^{1/2}$. Then $M=CC^T$. Conversely $x^TCC^Tx=\|C^Tx\|^2\ge0$. Deleting zero-eigenvalue columns gives a factor with exactly $\operatorname{rank}M$ columns. A symmetric square root $U\Lambda^{1/2}U^T$ is another choice; it should not be confused with an entrywise square root.

A Laplacian is positive semidefinite because $L=BB^T$ for the oriented incidence matrix $B$, or directly because $x^TLx=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i-x_j)^2$. This sum vanishes precisely when $x$ is constant on each connected component. Therefore a connected graph has one zero Laplacian eigenvalue and $n-1$ positive ones. Its adjacency matrix usually has negative eigenvalues, even though every adjacency entry is nonnegative.

Principal submatrices inherit positive semidefiniteness by zero extension, or by interlacing of their smallest eigenvalues. Consequently all their determinants are nonnegative. Checking only the leading principal determinants is insufficient for semidefiniteness: $\operatorname{diag}(0,-1)$ has leading determinants0 and0 but gives negative value on $e_2$. Its principal minor at index2 is−1. A necessary test must not be misstated as a sufficient one. We do not need a full principal-minor criterion for the arguments that follow.

7.7 A positive block matrix obeys a useful top-eigenvalue bound

Partition a positive semidefinite $M$ into two nonempty square diagonal blocks $M_{11},M_{22}$. We will prove

$$\lambda_1(M)\le\lambda_1(M_{11})+\lambda_1(M_{22}).$$

First justify a fact used in the factorization proof. For a rectangular $C$, the positive eigenvalues of $CC^T$ and $C^TC$ agree, including multiplicity. If $CC^Tx=\lambda x$ with $\lambda>0$, then $C^Tx\ne0$ because otherwise the left side would vanish; moreover $C^TC(C^Tx)=\lambda C^Tx$. The reverse map is $y\mapsto Cy$. On these eigenspaces the compositions multiply by $\lambda$, so the maps are isomorphisms after rescaling. Both matrices are symmetric, giving equality of their positive algebraic multiplicities as well. Zero multiplicities can differ when dimensions differ. If $C=0$, both top eigenvalues are0; otherwise their common largest positive eigenvalue is the same.

The recording also suggests a broader product fact. For $X$ of size $p\times q$ and $Y$ of size $q\times p$, eliminate either off-diagonal block in $\begin{pmatrix}I_p&tX\\-Y&I_q\end{pmatrix}$ by determinant-preserving block row operations. Eliminating the lower-left block gives determinant $\det(I_q+tYX)$; eliminating the upper-right gives $\det(I_p+tXY)$. Hence these determinants agree for every $t$. Substituting $t=-1/z$ and multiplying by powers of $z$ shows that $XY$ and $YX$ have the same nonzero characteristic roots with algebraic multiplicities; when $p\ne q$, only their zero multiplicities can differ. This determinant argument supplies the multiplicity statement that an eigenvector correspondence alone would not establish for general nonsymmetric products. For the Gram products used here, symmetry already made the eigenspace argument sufficient.

Write $M=CC^T$ and partition its rows as $C=\binom{C_1}{C_2}$. Then $M_{ii}=C_iC_i^T$ and $C^TC=C_1^TC_1+C_2^TC_2$. For any two symmetric matrices of the same size, the Rayleigh principle gives $\lambda_1(X+Y)\le\lambda_1(X)+\lambda_1(Y)$: evaluate each term on the same arbitrary unit vector, bound each separately, and maximize. Consequently

$$\lambda_1(M)=\lambda_1(C^TC)\le\lambda_1(C_1^TC_1)+\lambda_1(C_2^TC_2) =\lambda_1(M_{11})+\lambda_1(M_{22}).$$

Using a square $n\times n$ Gram factor avoids any empty-column convention in the zero case. A rectangular reduced factor gives the same result with zero treated separately. Equality is possible: $M=\binom{1}{2}(1,2)$ has top value5 and scalar diagonal blocks1 and4. Positive semidefiniteness matters: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has top value1 but both scalar diagonal blocks are0.

7.8 Shift by the smallest eigenvalue, then remove the shift

For an arbitrary real symmetric two-block $M$, set $s=\lambda_n(M)$. The matrix $M-sI$ is positive semidefinite because its eigenvalues are $\lambda_i-s\ge0$. Apply the preceding inequality to its two diagonal blocks:

$$\lambda_1(M)-s\le[\lambda_1(M_{11})-s]+[\lambda_1(M_{22})-s].$$

After rearrangement,

$$\boxed{\lambda_1(M)+\lambda_n(M)\le\lambda_1(M_{11})+\lambda_1(M_{22}).}$$

The shift must use the smallest eigenvalue; subtracting the largest generally makes the matrix negative semidefinite. The off-diagonal blocks are unchanged, and the identity sizes must match their respective diagonal blocks. A spectral shift is a simple operation with a precise sign, not a formal replacement of one matrix by another.

Now suppose $M$ is partitioned into $k\ge2$ nonempty groups with every diagonal block zero, not merely zero individual diagonal entries. Then

$$\boxed{\lambda_1(M)+(k-1)\lambda_n(M)\le0.}$$

For $k=2$, the two-block bound has right side0. Assume the claim for $k-1$ groups and retain the first $k-1$ groups as a principal submatrix $C$. The two-block bound for $M$, grouped as $(C,\text{last group})$, gives $\lambda_1(M)+\lambda_n(M)\le\lambda_1(C)$. The induction hypothesis gives $\lambda_1(C)+(k-2)\lambda_{\min}(C)\le0$. Interlacing gives $\lambda_n(M)\le\lambda_{\min}(C)$. Since $k-2\ge0$,

$$\lambda_1(M)+(k-1)\lambda_n(M) \le\lambda_1(C)+(k-2)\lambda_n(M) \le\lambda_1(C)+(k-2)\lambda_{\min}(C)\le0.$$

This proves the full induction, including the sign of the substitution. In an adjacency matrix, a zero diagonal block means its group is an independent set. A proper coloring supplies such groups, so the next chapter will turn this matrix bound into a lower bound on the number of colors. For a general symmetric matrix the block entries may be negative; the proof uses symmetry and the zero blocks, not entrywise positivity.

7.9 An original extension: normalize a partition before compressing

Partition the vertex set into nonempty cells $V_1,\ldots,V_r$ of sizes $n_1,\ldots,n_r$. Let $H$ be the indicator matrix, with $H_{vi}=1$ when $v\in V_i$. Its columns are orthogonal but have norms $\sqrt{n_i}$. Thus $H^TH=D=\operatorname{diag}(n_i)$, not $I$. The normalized indicator matrix is $S=HD^{-1/2}$, and the symmetric quotient compression is

$$B=S^TAS=D^{-1/2}H^TAHD^{-1/2},\qquad S^TS=I.$$

Its entry $B_{ij}$ is the total number of ordered adjacency incidences from cell $i$ to cell $j$, divided by $\sqrt{n_in_j}$. For distinct cells each edge is counted once in that directional sum; for a diagonal cell its internal edges are counted twice. These conventions follow directly from matrix multiplication. The eigenvalues of $B$ interlace those of $A$ by section7.4.

The raw average-neighbor quotient is $Q=D^{-1}H^TAH$. It is generally nonsymmetric, but $B=D^{1/2}QD^{-1/2}$, so it is similar to the symmetric $B$ and has real eigenvalues. Using the eigenvalues of $H^TAH$ itself would be a different, unnormalized compression and need not obey the same bounds.

A partition is equitable when every vertex of cell $i$ has the same number $Q_{ij}$ of neighbors in cell $j$, for every pair $i,j$. Exactly then $AH=HQ$, hence $AS=SB$. If $By=\beta y$, the nonzero lifted vector $Sy$ satisfies $A(Sy)=\beta(Sy)$, so each quotient eigenvalue is a genuine full-graph eigenvalue. The matrix equality also gives invariance of the cell-constant subspace. Conversely $AH=HQ$ reads those individual neighbor-count equations row by row, proving the equivalence.

Our running graph has twin vertices 1 and3. The partition $(\{0\},\{1,3\},\{2\},\{4\})$ is equitable, with

$$Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&1&0\\1&0&1&0\\1&2&0&1\\0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt2&1&0\\\sqrt2&0&\sqrt2&0\\1&\sqrt2&0&1\\0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The orthogonal missing direction $e_1-e_3$ has eigenvalue0 because the two adjacency columns are identical. The quotient retains the other four values: its characteristic polynomial is $z^4-6z^2-4z+2$, and the full polynomial is $z$ times it. This is an exact reduction backed by the invariant-subspace equality, not an assumption that every clustering preserves eigenvectors.

Contrast the two-cell partition $(\{0,1,2\},\{3,4\})$. Internal triangle degrees are2, the total cross-edge count is3, and the second cell has no internal edges. Thus $Q=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\3/2&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&\sqrt{3/2}\\\sqrt{3/2}&0\end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $1\pm\sqrt{5/2}$. The top value approximately2.581139 is a valid lower bound on $\rho(A)$, stronger here than the all-ones average-degree test2.4. But the first cell's cross-neighbor counts are $(1,0,2)$, and the second cell's are $(2,1)$; this partition is not equitable. Its compressed values are generally Ritz approximations, not exact retained eigenvalues.

7.10 Measure the defect of a lifted compressed mode

There is a direct way to test the last distinction. Define the defect $R=AS-SB$. For an eigenpair $By=\beta y$, the lifted residual is

$$A(Sy)-\beta(Sy)=Ry,\qquad \|Sy\|=\|y\|.$$

Expand $Sy$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $A$. The squared residual is $\sum_i|c_i|^2(\lambda_i-\beta)^2$, while $\sum_i|c_i|^2=\|y\|^2$. Therefore some full eigenvalue obeys

$$\operatorname{dist}(\beta,\operatorname{spec}A)\le\frac{\|Ry\|}{\|y\|}.$$

This is an absolute error bound, not an assertion that the nearest eigenvalue is the top one or that the corresponding eigenvector is close without a gap. In the equitable case $R=0$ and every lift is exact. In a nonequitable partition some individual compressed eigenvector can still have zero defect; equitability is the stronger statement that the entire compressed subspace is invariant.

Refining a partition enlarges the cell-constant subspace: any old cell-constant vector is constant on the smaller cells as well. The top Rayleigh maximum over the refined subspace cannot decrease, and remains at most the full maximum. This proves monotone improvement of the largest compressed lower bound under refinement, without promising a rate or that every additional group is equally informative.

7.11 Execute interlacing, Gram and quotient checks

This original listing tests every nonempty proper principal subset of the running graph numerically, verifies the quotient polynomial exactly, checks an explicit positive block example, and measures an imperfect lift. The generic theorems rest on the proofs above. Floating-point assertions include tolerances; exact symbolic equalities do not depend on those tolerances.

from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np
from sympy import Matrix, sqrt, symbols, expand

A=np.zeros((5,5))
for i,j in [(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(0,3),(0,2),(2,4)]: A[i,j]=A[j,i]=1
lam=np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)[::-1]
count=0
for m in range(1,5):
    for indices in combinations(range(5),m):
        beta=np.linalg.eigvalsh(A[np.ix_(indices,indices)])[::-1]
        assert np.all(beta <= lam[:m]+1e-12)
        assert np.all(beta >= lam[5-m:]-1e-12)
        count+=1
print(f"All {count} nonempty proper principal subsets interlace")
# An edge deletion is not a principal compression.
cycle=np.zeros((4,4))
for i,j in [(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,0)]:cycle[i,j]=cycle[j,i]=1
path=cycle.copy();path[0,3]=path[3,0]=0
assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(path)[-2] > np.linalg.eigvalsh(cycle)[-2]+.6
print("C4 to P4 edge deletion raises the second eigenvalue")
C=np.array([[1.,0.],[1.,1.],[0.,1.]])
G=C@C.T
bound=np.linalg.eigvalsh(G[:2,:2])[-1]+G[2,2]
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.eigvalsh(G)[-1],3)
assert 3 <= bound
print(f"PSD block bound: 3.000000 <= {bound:.6f}")
z=symbols('z')
B=Matrix([[0,sqrt(2),1,0],[sqrt(2),0,sqrt(2),0],
          [1,sqrt(2),0,1],[0,0,1,0]])
poly=expand(B.charpoly(z).as_expr())
assert poly == z**4-6*z**2-4*z+2
assert Matrix(A.astype(int)).charpoly(z).as_expr() == expand(z*poly)
H=np.zeros((5,4))
for col,cell in enumerate([[0],[1,3],[2],[4]]):H[cell,col]=1
S=H/np.sqrt(H.sum(axis=0))
assert np.allclose(A@S,S@np.array(B,dtype=float))
print("Twin quotient polynomial:",poly)
H2=np.zeros((5,2));H2[[0,1,2],0]=1;H2[[3,4],1]=1
S2=H2/np.sqrt(H2.sum(axis=0));B2=S2.T@A@S2
w,v=np.linalg.eigh(B2); beta=w[-1];y=v[:,-1]
res=np.linalg.norm(A@(S2@y)-beta*(S2@y))
assert np.isclose(beta,1+np.sqrt(2.5))
assert np.min(np.abs(lam-beta)) <= res+1e-12
assert beta < lam[0]
print(f"Two-cell top Ritz value: {beta:.6f}; lifted residual: {res:.6f}")
print("Principal compression, block bound and exact/approximate quotient checks passed")

7.12 Problems with complete solutions

  1. Use both interlacing endpoints. A symmetric matrix has spectrum $(7,4,1,-2,-5)$. Enclose the ordered spectrum of an arbitrary3-by-3 principal submatrix. Does the theorem force a positive or negative eigenvalue?

    Solution

    Here $n-m=2$. Thus $7\ge\beta_1\ge1$, $4\ge\beta_2\ge-2$, and $1\ge\beta_3\ge-5$. At least one positive eigenvalue is forced because $\beta_1\ge1$. A negative value is not forced: all three intervals permit a nonnegative ordered triple, for example $(3,2,0)$. The bounds alone do not assert that every permitted triple is realizable for a particular parent matrix. They are necessary constraints derived without its eigenvectors.

  2. A shared vector must be nonzero. Reconstruct the two subspace dimensions in each half of the interlacing proof. Why would replacing $n-k+1$ by $n-k$ break the first argument?

    Solution

    For the upper bound the dimensions are $k$ and $n-k+1$, summing to $n+1$. For the lower they are $m-k+1$ and $k+n-m$, again $n+1$. The dimension formula therefore gives an intersection of dimension at least1, allowing a nonzero vector with a defined Rayleigh quotient. If the first lower spectral span had dimension $n-k$, the sum would be $n$ and the lower bound on the intersection would be0. Two complementary subspaces can then intersect only in0, so no Rayleigh comparison follows. The index shift and the extra1 encode the same nonzero-intersection requirement.

  3. Do not infer PSD from leading zeros. Analyze $M=\operatorname{diag}(0,-1)$ and the graph Laplacian of a single edge. Which is PSD, and how do their entries differ from their quadratic behavior?

    Solution

    For $M$, $e_2^TMe_2=-1$, so it is not PSD even though its leading principal determinants are0 and0. The principal minor on the second index is−1. The single-edge Laplacian is $L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $x^TLx=(x_1-x_2)^2\ge0$, so it is PSD despite negative off-diagonal entries. Its eigenvalues are2 and0. Thus entry signs, a restricted minor list and positivity of a quadratic form are different tests.

  4. Rectangular Gram factors. For $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, find both Gram matrices and their spectra. Apply the two-block PSD bound with the first two rows in one block.

    Solution

    $C^TC=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues3 and1. Meanwhile $CC^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&2&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has the same positive values and one additional0, consistent with rank2. The first principal block is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ with top eigenvalue $(3+\sqrt5)/2$, and the second scalar block is1. Hence the bound reads $3\le(5+\sqrt5)/2\approx3.618034$. It is valid without being tight; the Gram argument does not require the two summands of $C^TC$ to share a maximizing vector.

  5. The shift has a sign. Let $M=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&-2\end{pmatrix}$. Verify the general two-block inequality and explain why subtracting the largest eigenvalue cannot justify the PSD step.

    Solution

    The characteristic polynomial is $z^2-13$, so the extremes are $\sqrt{13}$ and $-\sqrt{13}$. The scalar diagonal-block top values are2 and−2, summing to0, and $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=0$ attains equality. Subtracting $s=-\sqrt{13}$ gives eigenvalues $2\sqrt{13}$ and0, a PSD matrix to which the block proof applies. Subtracting $+\sqrt{13}$ instead gives0 and $-2\sqrt{13}$, a negative semidefinite matrix. Applying the PSD block theorem to it would violate its hypothesis.

  6. Symmetric spectrum or symmetric extremes? Explain why $K_{1,9}\sqcup K_3$ refutes the disconnected converse of $\lambda_1=-\lambda_n$. Give an odd trace that detects its nonbipartite component.

    Solution

    The star has eigenvalues3,−3 and eight0 values. The triangle adds2,−1,−1. The combined extremes are still3 and−3, yet the graph contains a triangle. Its third trace is $3^3+(-3)^3+2^3+(-1)^3+(-1)^3=6$. The six length3 closed walks are the triangle's three starting vertices in two orientations. A fully symmetric eigenvalue multiset would cancel every odd-power sum, so the full-spectrum criterion detects what the extreme pair misses.

  7. A block inequality before coloring. Partition $K_4$ into its four singleton cells and check $\lambda_1+(k-1)\lambda_n\le0$. Why cannot its vertices be put into two zero-diagonal blocks?

    Solution

    The spectrum is $(3,-1,-1,-1)$, so at $k=4$ the expression is $3+3(-1)=0$. A diagonal block must have all entries0, meaning no edges within its group. Any group containing two vertices of $K_4$ contains an edge. Thus every zero-diagonal block can contain at most one vertex, and two nonempty groups cannot cover all four vertices. If one incorrectly used $k=2$, the alleged inequality would read $3-1\le0$, which is false precisely because the hypothesized block structure does not exist.

  8. Normalize the twin partition. For the running graph's cells $\{0\},\{1,3\},\{2\},\{4\}$, explain the $\sqrt2$ quotient entries and identify the missing mode. Why is the raw average quotient asymmetric?

    Solution

    There are two edges from $\{0\}$ to the size2 twin cell, so the symmetric compression entry is $2/\sqrt{1\cdot2}=\sqrt2$. The same count applies from vertex 2 to that cell. In the raw average quotient, vertex 0 has two neighbors in the twin cell while each twin has one neighbor in $\{0\}$, giving $Q_{12}=2$ and $Q_{21}=1$. The size-weighted relation $B=D^{1/2}QD^{-1/2}$ restores symmetry. The cell-constant subspace has dimension4; its orthogonal complement is spanned by $e_1-e_3$. Identical adjacency columns make this a zero eigenvector. Equitability makes the compression invariant, so its four eigenvalues and this zero mode form the complete spectrum with multiplicity.

  9. An inexact quotient still carries evidence. For the two-cell partition in section7.9, find its top value and explain what a nonzero lifted residual establishes. Prove that splitting a cell cannot worsen the top compressed lower bound.

    Solution

    The symmetric2-by-2 quotient has characteristic polynomial $z^2-2z-3/2$, so the top value is $1+\sqrt{5/2}\approx2.581139$. This is below the full radius and exceeds the average-degree bound2.4. A residual norm $\varepsilon$ for a normalized lifted eigenvector guarantees that at least one full eigenvalue is within $\varepsilon$ of this value, by expansion in the full orthonormal eigenbasis. A nonzero residual means that particular lift is not an exact eigenvector; it does not by itself rule out an equal numerical eigenvalue elsewhere. On refinement, every old cell-constant vector remains in the new subspace. Maximizing the Rayleigh quotient over a superset cannot decrease its maximum, proving the monotone lower-bound improvement. Exact equality or a useful rate requires additional information.

7.13 Compression retains a space, not an entire graph

Interlacing is a consequence of symmetric quadratic forms and nonzero subspace intersections. Principal deletion, arbitrary edge deletion and grouping vertices impose different restrictions and must keep their own hypotheses. Positive semidefiniteness supplies a factorization and a block bound; a smallest-eigenvalue shift extends that bound to indefinite matrices without making the original matrix PSD.

A normalized quotient can retain exact modes when its subspace is invariant, or provide variational bounds and residual checks when it is not. The examples do not claim graph reconstruction from spectra or universal performance of a clustering method. The next chapter uses the zero-block inequality and interlacing to constrain coloring, cliques and independent sets.
